UX in AI

The role of user experience in AI solutions
The idea

An AI feature succeeds only when people can use its outputs to achieve meaningful goals, not merely when the model predicts accurately. UX covers expectations before use, interaction during use and the consequences after a recommendation is followed, so the whole socio-technical system is designed.

Why UX matters in AI solutions

  • AI-specific difficulty (slide 4): uncertain, sometimes plausible-but-wrong outputs create new interpretation, oversight and error-recovery demands.
  • Course perspective (slide 4): effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, decision quality, accessibility, safe reliance. A beautiful dashboard fails if it encourages an inappropriate recommendation.
  • Adoption is behavioural (slide 10): useful AI delivers no value if users cannot discover, understand or operate it. Interpretation: the interface can frame an uncertain recommendation as advice or as an apparently authoritative command. Action: users need to verify evidence, edit results, reject suggestions, recover from mistakes or request human assistance.
  • Consequences and context: a wrong music recommendation wastes attention, a wrong clinical one can cause harm; one output may help an advisor and confuse a first-year student.
  • Success requires evidence: measure the actual task, preserving agency, fairness and appropriate reliance.

UX in AI (slide 21): the total experience of interacting with systems whose behaviour is partly generated, predicted, inferred or adapted through AI: not only "can the user operate the interface?" but "does the user understand what the system is doing, why, and when to trust its output?"

Accuracy is not experienceTwo apps with the same 95% accuracy can differ completely in UX: the one that explains and lets users reject wrong predictions wins on interpretation, action and recovery, even if plainer (slide 3).

Interfaces, bad UX and Gestalt

Interface (slides 5, 7): a point of interaction between two systems or components that lets them communicate and exchange information, for example humans and technology; it lets software, hardware and systems work together, standardises interaction and ensures compatibility. Bad UX (slides 8, 34): Arngren (overcrowded), LINGsCARS (distracting animations), Gates N Fences (poor organisation).

Human-computer interaction (HCI)

HCI (slide 11): the human and the computer determine each other's behaviour over time, framed by the human's goals and tasks; design, evaluation and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use. Parts: user, computer or device, and task; the system must support the task (usability).

  • Human (slide 12): end-user, others in the organisation, cultural context; sensory (visual, auditory, haptic, spatial), acting (hands, voice, head, body), cognitive (perception, memory).
  • Computer (slide 12): hardware, software, input peripherals (keyboard, mouse, sensors) and output (screen, audio, haptics, VR/AR).
  • Interactive system (slide 13): the hardware, software, services and/or people users interact with to achieve a goal. Interaction: the user tells the computer what they want and it communicates results; users and their goals are the ultimate metric.

User experience

User experience (slide 20): how people interact with a product or service; all aspects of the end-user's interaction with the company, its services and products. Don Norman coined the term (slide 22): no product is an island; a cohesive set of experiences from first intentions to final reflections. UX design (slide 23): products people find easy and fun to use, considering their feelings; who, why, what and how. Context of use (slide 30): training, conventions, laws, competing products, human abilities and limitations.

The 4Cs of UX design

C3 is "Context" but talks about contentThe slide explains Context through content (text, images, videos, clickable elements). Do not confuse it with the "context of use" on slide 30.

The formal definition: ISO 9241-210

Not just "was it usable"The ISO wording covers anticipated use and the time before and after use, and it includes emotions, beliefs and comfort.

UX vs UI, and the myths about good design

User interface (slide 31): how people interact with products: the visual and informational elements and the navigational and organisational features. Per Nielsen, UI is the visual and interactive elements (buttons, icons, layouts); UX is the user's entire interaction with the product.

Four myths about good design (slides 33, 35): good design is just cool graphics, just common sense, left to marketing to understand customer needs, or fixable at the end.

  • Graphics are part of a bigger picture of what to communicate.
  • Many bad websites show it is not just common sense.
  • Marketing does not show behaviour: what people say versus do.
  • Late fixes overlook the cost of major changes; good design includes the right feature.

Human-AI collaboration (HAIC)

HAIC (slides 37, 39): humans and AI working together to achieve optimal outcomes, a cooperation model in which both leverage their strengths to jointly complete tasks. Human creativity, empathy and contextual judgment plus AI's speed, precision and data-processing power. Slide 36 swaps the centre from algorithms and AI to humans.

Human-centered AI (HCAI)

HCAI (slide 41, IBM 2026): an emerging discipline creating AI systems that amplify and augment rather than displace human abilities. It seeks to preserve human control so that AI meets our needs while operating transparently, delivering equitable outcomes and respecting privacy. Spiral: Understanding, Design and Development, Evaluation, with user involvement throughout.

Stakeholders (slide 43, picture only): users, researchers, developers, business leaders and policy-makers.

Control and automation are not a trade-offThe axes are independent. The goal is the top-right corner, not a point on the line between human control and automation; the grey bands mark excess of either.

Attributes (slide 46, word cloud). Largest three: reliable, safe, trustworthy. Smaller: predictable, private, secure, unbiased, fair, controllable, directable, usable, observable, comprehensible, explainable, transparent, interpretable, dependable, robust, agile, resilient, available, traceable, auditable.

Why HCAI is essential (slide 44): it bridges technology's potential and human needs: learning from human interaction; human-machine collaboration; adaptability and personalization; intuitive interfaces and accessibility; ethics and trust.

HCI vs HAIC, ethics and privacy

  • HCI vs HAIC (slide 47): design AI with users in mind: not only technically proficient, but user-friendly, ethical and trust-building.
  • Ethics (slide 48): can AI treat all users fairly? Is its decision mechanism transparent? Both affect trust.
  • Privacy and security (slide 49): AI processes large amounts of data, so protecting privacy and avoiding leaks is urgent (smart homes, smart medical care).
Questions the lecture asksUX in AI

    Model answer: B. Accuracy is equal, so the difference is interpretation (advice with a reason) and action (verify, reject, recover), per slide 10. A can push users to accept a wrong output with no way back (slide 4).
  • End-of-discussion question (slide 50): a university AI course recommender overlooks prerequisites; students accept recommendations at once with no explanation or warning. Redesign it for transparency, decision quality, user control and overall UX.

AI architecture

Architecture for scalable and user-centered AI systems
The idea

An AI system is several cooperating components, not a model bolted to an interface, and the way they are organised decides the experience (speed, reliability, how much users can understand and control). User expectations such as fast, reliable and trustworthy must be turned into measurable architectural requirements.

Why architecture determines the experience of AI

AI system architecture: how data sources, models, application services, interfaces and infrastructure are organised to deliver intelligent functionality and how they communicate and respond (slide 5). Slide 7: faster response improves UX, higher reliability builds trust, greater explainability increases understanding and confidence.

Architecture determines (slide 6): how fast your product learns from users, how easily models integrate with your data, how quickly feedback becomes improvement. An accurate model can still give an unsatisfactory experience if predictions arrive too late to support meaningful decisions (slide 5). Pattern benefits: reusability, scalability, reliability, maintainability, interview readiness.

AI systems as socio-technical architectures

AI solutions operate within workflows and can shape people, policies, responsibilities and environments (slide 8): interpretation, data quality, decision authority, procedures and operational constraints decide whether they do good, not only accuracy. They should fit different expertise, risk tolerance and confidence and support oversight, feedback and responsible decisions.

AI capability concerns (slide 11)AI usability concerns (slide 11)
predict, classify, generate, recommendunderstand, complete tasks, recover from mistakes, exercise appropriate control
Capability is not usabilityA system can predict, classify, generate and recommend well and still fail users who cannot understand it, finish their task, recover from mistakes or control it.

From UX principles to architectural requirements

UX principles (slide 10): usability, accessibility, responsiveness, transparency and trust. They become requirements: responsiveness needs efficient inference and resource allocation; transparency needs evidence retrieval, explanation services or model-output metadata; accessibility needs multiple input and output modalities.

Architectural design (slide 13): (1) usability as engineering requirements, e.g. a percentile of requests within an agreed time; (2) transparency: evidence retrieval, feature attribution, model metadata; (3) accessibility shapes interfaces and workflow; (4) trust and reliability: dependable, predictable, communicating limitations; (5) evaluation: the interface alone cannot show needs are met; add infrastructure monitoring, model evaluation, safety analysis, real-task measurements.

User-centered design, stakeholders and requirements

Human-centered design (slide 14): users' needs, abilities and expectations at the centre; start from user goals, not technologies; users join requirements, prototype evaluation and usability testing; improve from feedback. Different users (slide 15), university assistant: students need useful answers, clear navigation; administrators update tools; technical staff deployment, monitoring, maintenance; security staff audit information, access control.

Requirements (slide 16)

  • Functional: what it must do (answer, retrieve).
  • Nonfunctional: how well: scalability, security, availability, response time.
  • Operational: infrastructure, maintenance, security, responsibilities.
  • Responsible-AI: privacy, transparency, fairness, accountability, human oversight.

Capabilities needing support (slides 18, 19)

  • Explainability: evidence retrieval, feature attribution, explanation generation.
  • Accessibility: text, speech, screen readers.
  • Human control: review, correction, approval, escalation.
  • Personalisation: adapts to preferences and context.
  • Feedback: correct outputs, report problems.
Three or four?Slide 15 says "three categories", but slide 16 lists four: functional, nonfunctional, operational, responsible-AI. Learn all four.

Architectural decisions and UX trade-offs

Small application: modular monolith; large platform: independently scalable services (slide 20).

Do not maximise every metricMore computational capacity may cut response times but raises operating cost and energy consumption (slide 21); judge against actual user needs.

Architectural qualities

  • Scalable vs user-centred (slide 23): scalable takes growing workloads, data volumes and computation; user-centred organises components around users' needs, abilities and contexts. Both are essential: an assistant good for 100 students may crawl for thousands.
  • Elasticity (slide 24): a cloud chatbot adds inference capacity during exams. Adaptability (slide 25): modular interfaces and well-defined data contracts let you replace a model without redesigning the application.

The five layers of a user-centered AI system

Application layer (slide 29): coordinates interface, data services, AI models and external systems. An API is a defined mechanism for sending requests and receiving responses. Orchestration coordinates processing steps and sets the order services run (retrieval, calculations, database queries, content generation).

AI processing and inference

Lifecycle (slide 30): training creates parameters from datasets; evaluation decides whether requirements are met; a model registry stores approved versions and metadata; deployment makes a model available for inference. Treat training and inference as distinct workloads; controlled updates reduce production risk. Inference (slide 31): at scale, multiple servers, GPUs, load balancing, request scheduling; distributed inference uses multiple instances.

The data layer, generative AI and RAG

Data architecture (slide 32): how information enters and becomes available (databases, object storage, document repositories, pipelines, search indexes). Embedding models turn text into numerical vectors. Retrieval quality depends on document preparation, indexing, search methods and access controls; manage updates, quality, provenance and public versus restricted information.

Observability and evaluating the architecture for UX

Evaluation methods (slide 42): load testing, performance monitoring, usability testing, accessibility assessments, feedback analysis. Satisfy both technical and user-experience metrics; technical metric → user-centred counterpart:

  • response time and latency → perceived responsiveness

  • error and failure rates → task interruptions and recovery experience
  • inference and retrieval performance → quality and usefulness of AI responses
  • resource utilisation and cost → sustainable service quality

Technologies and frameworks for web front-end development

Front-end tech · what runs in the browser, and what it does to the user's experience
The idea

The front end is what the user sees and touches. It is built from HTML (content), CSS (presentation) and JavaScript (behaviour), and frameworks, rendering strategies and performance measures sit on top. New technology appears when user needs outgrow the old one, but a newer technology does not automatically give a better experience: it must be judged against complexity and the real requirements.

How a web application works

  • Web development: creating and maintaining websites and web applications: designing user interfaces, processing data, connecting components. HTTP defines how requests and responses are exchanged.
  • Status codes: 200 OK success; 400 Bad Request client-side request problem; 500 Internal Server Error server failure.
  • Roles: front-end, back-end, full-stack (slide 9). Benefits (slide 10): secure, scalable, speedy development; reactive, responsive apps; realtime, interactive interface; cross-platform, cross-browser; robust environment.
The browser is not a trusted placeUsers can inspect and modify the code and requests in their own browser. So sensitive credentials, authorisation decisions and official eligibility checks must be enforced by protected backend services, never only in front-end code.

Accessibility, semantic HTML and forms

Accessibility means a site is usable by people of all abilities. The W3C's WCAG standards are described as a legal requirement that also improves SEO, brand value and market share. Four principles, POUR (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust):

PrincipleWhat it requires
Perceivablecontent available through sight, hearing and/or touch
Operablekeyboard-accessible, navigable, compatible with different input methods
Understandablereadable and predictable, with clear labels and instructions
Robustworks with a variety of assistive technologies, browsers and devices

Semantic HTML gives content meaning for browsers and assistive technologies; a native button has built-in keyboard and focus behaviour.

Forms: prevent errors early

  • Form inputs: text, checkboxes, radio buttons, selection lists, submit buttons. Client-side validation checks required data before the request is sent.
  • Browser-only validation is insufficient for security: requests can be built independently of the page, so the server must check too.

Good error handling

  • An error message identifies the problem and explains how to correct it.
  • When validation fails, keep the correctly entered values.
  • A validated form does not prove AI recommendations are correct.

HTML, CSS, JavaScript and the DOM

  • HTML = content and meaning; CSS = presentation; JavaScript = behaviour; DOM = tree of nodes. The DOM (Document Object Model) is a programming interface: the page as nodes and objects that programs can change.
  • Events: a click, tap, drag, text entry or form submission creates an event; JavaScript registers event listeners that run functions. Slide 21: chart click opens filtered records; data fetch, success alert; radio button, list filter; page load, cookie modal.

Where each goes wrong: HTML, a native control rebuilt badly (keyboard and focus behaviour lost); CSS, low contrast, small text, hidden focus indicators, misleading visual grouping; JavaScript, extra complexity, loading cost, more places for errors.

CSS: visual hierarchy, responsive layout, modern CSS

Visual hierarchy arranges elements so the most important stand out; consistent presentation shows relationships and actions. Tools (slide 16): size, colour, contrast, alignment, repetition, whitespace, texture, style. Slide image: symmetry and order, similarity, proximity, continuity, closure, common region.

From static pages to dynamic applications

A static website serves prepared resources (HTML, CSS, JavaScript, images), not necessarily a personalised document per request. Advantages: fast loading, predictable navigation, minimal JavaScript; suits users who want stable information. Disadvantages: complex interaction needs browser code, external APIs or server processing; no inherent personalised server-side processing.

Direct DOM manipulation works for small interfaces but coordinating many linked updates gets hard, which pushed developers to component and state-management abstractions.

Modern rendering and Progressive Web Apps

SSG = HTML produced in advance, at build; SSR = HTML generated on the server and sent to the browser; CSR = JavaScript in the browser builds much of the interface; hydration = client-side JavaScript connects behaviour to already-rendered HTML.

A PWA uses available browser capabilities (installability, offline access, device integration); offline helps users with unstable connectivity. A web app manifest holds name, icons and display preferences for app-like installation.

Measuring real UX: LCP, INP and CLS

Do not mix up the threeLCP = largest element rendered (loading); INP = feedback after an interaction (responsiveness), not first load; CLS = unexpected movement (stability), which can make reading hard or trigger a wrong control.

Security, privacy and streaming AI output

  • Users can inspect and modify downloaded JavaScript, form values and network requests: secret API keys and privileged credentials must not be embedded in client-side JavaScript (slide 47).
  • Untrusted information rendered as executable HTML can introduce Cross-Site Scripting (XSS): AI text and API strings are untrusted, so render text as text and sanitise carefully only for rich content.
  • Data minimisation: collect only what the task needs; safeguards must be understandable.
  • Streaming (slide 48): earlier visible feedback instead of waiting for the full response. Streamed text must not be confused with a finalised or verified result. Streams can stop (connectivity loss, service failure, cancellation): show incomplete output, offer retry or continuation.

User-centred design principles

User-centred design (UCD) · research the users first, then design, test and repeat
The idea

User-centred design lets the users' characteristics, needs, goals, tasks and contexts guide every design decision, so that systems help people reach meaningful goals effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily, not just deliver technically correct functions. It runs as a cycle: understand the context of use, specify requirements, produce designs, evaluate them with users, and iterate.

What UCD is, and why AI needs it

  • Definition: the intended users' characteristics, needs, goals, tasks and contexts guide design decisions throughout development.
  • For AI: predictions must be presented through interfaces users can understand, evaluate and control.
  • Evidence over assumptions: decisions come from observations, interviews and usability evaluations, not developers' assumptions.
  • Philosophy (slide 8): investigate real user problems before choosing a technology; representative users contribute throughout (research, observation, prototype evaluation, usability testing); account for human limits (perception, memory, attention, physical interaction, accessibility); repeat evaluation and refinement.

Design goals and usability goals

A design goal says what the system should help users accomplish. Usability is how effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily specified users achieve specified goals in a particular context of use.

Stage 3: design solutions and iterative design

  • Ideas become sketches, wireframes or initial prototypes, revised on user feedback; the result is a prototype that addresses the identified requirements.
  • Iterative design repeatedly develops, evaluates and improves a solution based on evidence from users and constraints. Each iteration begins with a clear objective or hypothesis.
  • Prototypes let teams test ideas before full implementation.

Stage 4: evaluate with usability testing

Usability testing evaluates how representative users interact with an interface on realistic tasks, to find problems in the design, not to judge participants' intelligence or competence. For AI also check understanding of recommendations, appropriate reliance, user control, error recovery.

Planning (slide 48)

  • State what to evaluate; pick users who reflect the audience; write scenarios from real goals, without saying which buttons to click.
  • Prepare prototype, instructions, consent, observation methods, completion criteria.

Conducting (slide 49)

  • Explain the purpose, get informed consent, reassure that the interface, not the person, is evaluated.
  • Tasks as real goals without revealing controls or steps; no leading questions, no hints, only neutral prompts.
  • Record actions, hesitation, errors, repeated attempts, help requests, outcomes.

Prioritising problems and the next iteration (slides 51-52)

  1. Identify problems: confusing navigation, unclear AI recommendations, errors, failed tasks.
  2. Assess severity by frequency, impact on task completion, consequences for users, difficulty of recovery.
  3. Prioritise: consequential errors and blocked tasks before minor visual inconsistencies.
  4. Plan improvements (explanations, navigation, error prevention, feedback) and re-test for new problems.

Applying design thinking to AI projects · understand people before you choose the technology
The idea

Design thinking is a human-centred way to solve complex, poorly defined problems. It runs through Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test and Iterate, and it asks first whether AI is needed at all. Model accuracy is not the same as a good user experience, so every AI concept is tested with real tasks and revised from evidence.

Why design thinking matters in AI projects

Design thinking brings human needs, empathy, insights; AI brings data, pattern recognition, computation. Together: human-centred AI innovation (slide 15), with four gains: deeper problem understanding (contextual research plus data); more relevant AI applications (real human problems, not technology-first); better UX and adoption (clear, intuitive, trustworthy interaction); responsible AI development (stakeholders, bias, consequences considered early).

  • Model performance and UX are different dimensions. Accuracy, precision and recall describe the model; UX asks whether users accomplish meaningful tasks. "High accuracy means a good AI product" is false.
  • Support the user's decision process; AI adds uncertainty.
  • Ask whether AI is appropriate at all: if predictable filtering solves the problem, machine learning adds complexity without enough benefit.
  • IBM: design thinking and generative AI are complementary.

Empathize: understand people before designing AI

  • Separate user goals from requested features: a student asking for an AI chatbot may really need reliable guidance or a faster way to compare courses.
  • Context shapes needs; the existing experience reveals opportunities. Include diverse users and stakeholders.
  • Unmet needs: one professional's problem is a lead, not a rule. 12 working learners, 9 report trouble comparing course schedules: a descriptive result, not evidence that 75% of all working learners have the problem.
  • Users expect (slide 18): trust, privacy, transparency, control. Trust: know capabilities and limits (no blind acceptance, no needless rejection). Transparency: support decisions without technical overload. Privacy: affects willingness to share data. Control: modify, compare, reject, correct. Design for inappropriate reliance and errors.

Define: from insights to a design problem

A good problem definition describes a meaningful user difficulty, is specific enough to guide decisions, stays open to different solutions, and includes context and consequences: Who, what, and why (slide 23 adds where). User problem = unmet need or obstacle; technical solution = proposed means of addressing it.

  • POV statement (backed by research evidence). User: whose problem. Need: outcome desired. Insight: links the difficulty to circumstances, motivations or observed patterns.
  • HMW: How = possible approaches; Might = experiment, uncertainty; We = collaboration of developers, designers, advisers, administrators, users. E.g. "How might we help working professionals identify courses that fit their available study time?"
Solution-first framingStarting from "we must build an LLM" restricts creativity: simpler, cheaper solutions get overlooked, and a poorly defined problem leads to inappropriate optimisation. Good framing identifies the decision or activity that needs support.

Ideate: generate and select AI solution concepts

Multimodal UX

Multimodal UX design · voice, touch and gesture, chosen and coordinated
The idea

Multimodal UX is not about adding more ways to interact. It is about selecting, combining and coordinating voice, touch, gesture and feedback so users can work with an AI system naturally, reliably and accessibly. Each modality has its own strengths and failure modes, so the design must handle context, conflicts, fallbacks, devices and accessibility.

What multimodal design is

  • Definition: UI experiences with several modes of interaction such as voice, text or haptic feedback, to improve interaction and engagement.
  • Voice suits natural-language requests, touch direct control of visible elements, contactless gestures places where touching is inconvenient. Noise, lighting, device size, motor abilities, privacy and attention decide.
  • Modalities work independently, sequentially or simultaneously; a smartphone alone offers touchscreen, microphone, speakers, haptic motor.
  • Success needs meaningful coordination: preserve context, interpret commands, consistent feedback, let users continue when a modality becomes unavailable.
  • Why it matters in AI: more ways to express intent; modalities for different conditions (voice when hands are busy, touch in noise or for privacy); diverse abilities.

Main design concern: voice = speech errors, dialogue; touch = target accuracy, discoverability; gesture = recognition reliability, fatigue.

Voice-based UX

A voice user interface (VUI) works through spoken language; hands-free and eyes-free, command-based or conversational. Weak for much detail; adds uncertainty in recognition and interpretation.

Assistant blocks (slide 14): voice input, language understanding, third-party services, response generation, authentication, task and user feedback.

  • Commands: users' natural words; linguistic variation; say what can be requested; concise responses; separate consequential commands.
  • Conversation: clear cues that speech was detected. Barge-in lets users interrupt system speech; the system must tell intentional speech from background talk and its own audio.
  • Feedback: confirmation proportional to importance; audio cues for simple states; long results disclosed progressively; coordinated with visual, tactile feedback.
  • Recognition errors: the transcript differs from speech (word replaced, omitted, added); interpretation errors occur even with a right transcript. Distinguish uncertainty from failure: clarify when ambiguous; repeated failures must change strategy; protect important actions. Evaluate with both transcription and task measures.
Word Error Rate (standard ASR metric)WER = (S + D + I) / N × 100%S = substitutions · D = deletions · I = insertions · N = words in the reference transcript

Challenges: noise; languages, accents, styles; voice-only inaccessible; microphone privacy. If the microphone is unreliable, offer touch, text or another alternative while preserving the user's progress.

Course assistant (slide 19): "Find online business courses available in the evenings" = Subject Business, Format Online, Schedule Evening; brief spoken summary plus visible cards; user continues or taps.

Touch-based UX

Direct manipulation links actions and objects intuitively; the touchscreen combines input and output. Gestures (slide 21): tap selects or activates; swipe moves through content (never require discovering an invisible swipe for essential content); drag moves items or adjusts controls (offer an alternative when precision is hard); pinch scales or zooms; long press reveals contextual actions. Follow platform conventions.

Four principles (slide 22): target size and spacing; clarity and visual hierarchy; typography and readability for standing users; navigation and wayfinding. Users decide within about three seconds whether to explore or abandon.

  • Target size: Android at least 48 x 48 density-independent pixels; Apple 44 x 44 px (slide 24). Do not mix up with WCAG 2.2 Level AA: 24 x 24 CSS pixel minimum with exceptions. Spacing prevents accidental activation.
  • Feedback: haptic (short vibration) reinforces a selection; delayed acknowledgement makes users repeat actions and create duplicate requests; important errors not only by colour, sound or vibration.

Fixes (slide 27): secret swipe-left delete = visible Remove with Undo; small Save and Delete side by side = larger, separated controls; unclear selected state = persistent highlight; drag-only reordering = drag plus Move Up/Down; immediate irreversible action = review and confirmation where appropriate.

Preventing problems (slide 26): accidental activation, hidden gestures, ambiguous critical movements; touch cancellation, simple alternatives, undo.

Gesture-based and contactless interaction

Touch gestures need contact with a surface. Contactless gestures need none: a raised hand, pointing direction or lateral movement seen by cameras or motion sensors. Useful at public kiosks, museum displays, industrial interfaces.

  • Mappings (slide 31): hand right = next card; left = previous; pointing pose = visible focus; open palm = pause or cancel.
  • Design: consistent gesture-action link; manageable vocabulary; comfortable; show when recognised; separate recognition from execution when errors matter; keep alternatives (touch, buttons, accessible navigation).
  • Challenges: sensors, lighting; fatigue; unintentional gestures cause false actions; varies by user; privacy, accessibility.

Choosing the right modality

Start from the task (language search = speech; choosing among visible options = touch). Weigh information complexity, precision (text entry, value adjustment, small targets are hard by contactless gesture), device capabilities, environment. Users keep meaningful choice.

Interaction patterns and input fusion

  • When: sequential = different modalities at different stages of a task; simultaneous = related information at about the same time.
  • Relation: complementary = different pieces of one intended action; redundant = similar information via several modalities; independent = no fusion needed.

Multimodal fusion combines speech, touch, gesture to determine intent; the pattern decides how.

Multimodal AI pipeline (slide 37), eight steps: ingest inputs; preprocess; encode each modality; align signals (time, space, meaning); fuse (early, late, attention or cross-modal, hybrid); reason or classify; generate output; evaluate (accuracy, confidence, relevance, safety and bias check, human review, feedback loop).

Context, conflicts, switching and fallback

  • Interaction context holds what is needed to interpret the current request; system state is the application's condition. Context lets users switch modality without repeating. Referential commands ("compare these two") need synchronised context.
  • Conflict: the student says "Select Course A" while tapping Course B. Prioritise deliberate direct selection, but never auto-submit an enrolment when voice and touch name different courses. Feedback must not contradict; state the recognised action and its result; if unsure, show alternatives.
  • Modality switching changes how users interact while continuing the same task, preserving context. Fallback is an alternative when a modality becomes unsuitable or unavailable, without forced repetition. Switching must be visible. Accessibility requires equivalent task functionality; WCAG 2.2 covers concurrent input mechanisms and unrestricted input methods.

Cross-device consistency and adaptive interfaces

  • Layouts need not be identical: task concepts keep their meaning and state is preserved. Shared devices: personal and sensitive information must not automatically appear on a public kiosk.
  • Adaptive interfaces adjust to capabilities and context (smartwatch: short notifications; tablet: detailed comparisons); users should understand and control important adaptations: clear reason, reversible.
  • Slide 44: unreliable speech = touch or text; small display = shorter summary; public device = limit personal information; microphone off = non-voice; no gesture tracking = accessible controls.

Accessibility, privacy and environment

  • Abilities: sensory, motor, cognitive and communication.
  • Essential functions must not depend only on speech; spoken instructions, confirmations and errors need a non-audio form.
  • Contactless gesture must not be the only way to complete essential tasks (limited arm movement, tremors, fatigue): allow touch, keyboard, switch or other input. Complex touch gestures need simpler alternatives.
  • Accessibility must hold when switching modalities.
  • Privacy: voice captures spoken content; camera gestures capture movements and surroundings. Show when sensing is active; collect only what the function needs. Social acceptability affects adoption; noise, lighting, distance, space affect reliability.

Evaluating multimodal UX

  • Task completion across modalities and combinations; Word Error Rate for speech; precision, recall for gesture detection; time for recognition and recovery.
  • A modality good in ideal conditions becomes inefficient if users keep correcting or switching.
  • Include modality switching, conflict resolution and fallback effectiveness. Takeaway: the most appropriate modality, with consistency, accessibility and control.

AR & VR

Augmented and virtual reality · designing for space, body and comfort
The idea

AR adds digital content to the real world, VR replaces most of it with a simulation, and MR stresses how tightly the two interact. Good immersive UX is not realistic graphics: it is meaningful tasks, reliable spatial alignment, comfort, safety and user control. AI helps by recognising objects and guiding users, but the human must stay in control.

AR, VR, MR and XR: what each one is

AR enhances perception of the real, physical world with computer-generated content. VR simulates an alternate world users explore and interact with (computers, sensors, headsets, gloves). XR is the umbrella term for technologies combining or simulating physical and digital experiences, commonly AR, VR, MR; boundaries vary.

CharacteristicAugmented realityVirtual reality
Primary environmentPhysical, with digital contentPredominantly simulated
Main UX challengeAligning content with realityMaintaining immersion and comfort
Physical awarenessUsually maintained, maybe obstructedOften substantially reduced
Spatial interactionPhysical and virtual objectsSimulated objects and environments
University exampleCampus navigationLaboratory training
Do not mix them upA completely simulated lab is VR, not AR or MR; MR = spatial integration of physical and digital.

Real-world applications and the functions of AR

ApplicationTypeBenefits
Maintenance (industry)ARContextual instructions, component identification, fewer errors, safety
Laboratory training (education)VRSafe hands-on practice, repeatable experiments, real-time feedback, skills
Architectural visualisation (architecture and engineering)VRExplore designs in 3D, scale and layout, collaborative review, early improvements
Immersive museum (culture and entertainment)AREnriched exhibits, 3D models, interactive storytelling, engagement

Also navigation, healthcare visualisation, smart retail. Three AR functions: visualisation (see into hard-to-see things: live vein images), instruction (real-time information, diagrams), interaction (virtual control panels replace physical controls).

New UX considerations: opportunities and challenges

Account for spatial orientation, depth perception, viewpoint, virtual-object placement, user movement, physical comfort and environmental safety. AI: computer vision identifies objects; virtual agents guide or adapt information. Aim: meaningful tasks, alignment, comfort, control. The most impressive experience is not necessarily the most useful, especially for precise or consequential tasks.

Opportunities

  • 3D shows locations and functions better than 2D diagrams; experiential learning.

Challenges

  • New interaction requirements: apparent distance, reach, response to movement.
  • AR depends on understanding the environment: poor lighting, few features, reflective surfaces, tracking failures make objects drift or sit wrong.
  • VR must manage comfort: bad locomotion, unstable rendering, unnatural motion, long sessions.

Enabling technology: hardware, degrees of freedom and displays

  • VR hardware: head-mounted display (HMD), motion tracking sensors, input controllers, computing unit.
  • AR hardware: transparent displays (or camera passthrough), cameras, sensors, real-time rendering; phones and tablets are entry points.
  • Hardware shapes UX: resolution, refresh rate, field of view, weight, tracking coverage, input precision, battery, physical space.

Stereoscopic display: slightly offset images to each eye mimic binocular vision, creating 3D depth (generally 1-5 minutes of arc per pixel). Field of view: angular extent of the visible scene. Lens distortion, blur, limited resolution, convergence-focal mismatch hurt comfort.

Spatial tracking, SLAM and performance

Spatial tracking: sensors, cameras, algorithms give real-time position, orientation, movement. Sensor fusion: several sensors merged into one estimate more accurate, complete and robust than any single one (LiDAR points thin out with range, scatter in fog).

SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping) maps an environment while tracking the system's own position. Challenges: computational complexity, sensor noise and calibration, environment, real-time limits, scalability, fusion, hardware limits, drift.

FPS is among the most critical determinants of VR quality (environment complexity, hardware, rendering, latency). Latency, the delay between action and response, is detrimental: minimise it.

UX design for AR

Environmental design: the context where users meet the app; the device is a "window" into an augmented environment (street, concert, driving); it guides UI placement, colour, size. Interaction design: interaction inside the window; context limits it too.

  • Anchoring. World-anchored content stays tied to its target (lab-instrument label); not all information should be fixed. Allow for tracking uncertainty.
  • Spatial layout. Distances suited to the task; readability needs more than font size; minimise head and neck movement.
  • Clutter. Arrange overlays by spatial constraints; emphasise the attended scene, others abstract; use focus+context.
  • Guidance for real tasks. Link instructions to physical actions in manageable steps (identify component, next step, which part); arrows, highlights, labels separate the intended part from alternatives; support progress tracking and correction. Keep AI-generated guidance separate from authoritative procedural requirements. Never obstruct hazards, reduce situational awareness, or push users beyond training or authorisation.

UX design for VR

Support a clear activity, not just exploration: spatial coherence, understandable object rules, realism balanced with instructional use, clear entry, orientation, progression and exit.

Spatial orientation: knowing your position and direction in the virtual space. Use landmarks (e.g. recognisable lab workstations), let environment structure signal navigation options, scale aids to complexity, help recovery, judge wayfinding by observable outcomes.

Presence vs immersionPresence is the user's state; immersion is the technology's capability. An examiner may swap them.

Spatial interaction, selection and manipulation

Spatial interaction communicates intentions in 3D: gaze-based, hand tracking (direct manipulation), motion controllers, body movement (navigation, embodiment). Choose by spatial task.

Manipulation design: match technique to activity; object properties influence design; feedback shows when an object can be manipulated and the consequences; precise tasks need assistance (the snap).

Human-AI collaboration, AI assistance and human control

The goal is effective cooperation, not maximum automation; the level depends on the task and the cost of failure.

  • AI assistance: vision identifies objects, agents support learning, adapts to task state. It can be wrong: separate verified instructions from uncertain suggestions.
  • Human control (vital when guidance affects physical action): confirmation before significant actions, reliable interruption, communicated uncertainty, recovery to a safe state.
Slide exampleAn AR assistant identifies a component with low recognition confidence: the interface asks the technician to verify the component before showing component-specific instructions.

Accessibility, comfort, safety and privacy

Accessibility. Essential tasks must not depend only on complex movement; offer alternative channels, adjustable settings, orientation recovery; test with diverse users. W3C XR needs: alternative input, orientation, motion-independent interaction, customisation, sickness avoidance.

Comfort and safety: also physical and psychological discomfort, fatigue, physical risks. Privacy and ethics: devices may process the user's physical environment and collect head poses, controller movements, hand positions, activity logs; eye tracking adds concerns; spatial data may allow inferences about personal characteristics (W3C WebXR: movement, height).

Evaluating AR/VR user experience

Combine technical reliability with UX evidence. Measures: task effectiveness (meaningful goals achieved?), spatial accuracy (positioning, alignment), presence (subjective experience), comfort (symptoms, physical demands). Worked example:

Personalisation & trends

Balancing personalisation with user preferences · and how UX research is changing
The idea

An adaptive AI system tailors the experience to each person, so the designer now governs the behaviour of a changing experience instead of drawing fixed screens. Personalisation must be balanced with user-defined constraints, control, privacy and variety, and it must be evaluated with scorecards, experiments and new research methods. Success is measured by what people can meaningfully and responsibly achieve, not by one impressive metric.

From static interfaces to adaptive AI experiences

Static interfaces give predefined behaviour; adaptive AI interfaces change outputs from user information and context, so action and response are less clearly linked. Researchers must study both human behaviour and AI system behaviour; the objective shifts to governing the behaviour of a changing experience.

Personalisation vs customisation: who is in control?

Personalisation tailors experience, content and recommendations to an individual without manual input (proactive, data-driven): industry content from browsing, products from purchase history, shows from viewing habits. Customisation: the user manually adjusts settings or layout (widgets, dark/light mode, filters), building satisfaction and ownership.

FactorPersonalisationCustomisation
Trust and transparencyDepends on user data and algorithmsFeels safer: users consciously make changes
ScalabilityScales across millions of usersRelies on individual effort
Business impactEngagement, conversions, loyaltyRetention via choice
The personalisation loopBenefit: less manual setup, relevance, engagement, scale. Critique: it is self-reinforcing: content matching past behaviour traps users in a loop, reducing exposure to new ideas.

Explicit and implicit preferences, user models and preference controls

Explicit (declared)Implicit (behavioural)

LimitsIncomplete; biased (look better, no time, distrust); needs effort; may be outdatedNeeds fine analysis; misread (a visit is not interest); privacy and compliance; ambiguous

Course Planner: explicit: online delivery, max 6 study hours a week, analytics goal. Inferred: compares Python courses, saves project-based options, possible analytics interest. Hybrid: respect declared restrictions, update as the user corrects signals.

User model: structured representation of what matters for an individual: goals (what they want to accomplish), preferences (desirable solution characteristics), constraints (requirements recommendations must respect), context (circumstances of use); open to correction. Preference controls: inspect stored preferences; predictable response to edits; reset from unwanted personalisation; temporary separate from persistent changes.

Cold start: no prior data, so no meaningful personalised results from learned behaviour. User: a new user has no history (initial preference questions help). Item: a new product, content item or creative has not been seen by enough users. System: a new platform is deployed with no historical data at all.

Short-term intent: what the user is trying to do now; long-term preferences: persistent goals or interests. They can conflict (session-aware personalisation stresses recent context; long-term models give continuity); combining needs an explicit weighting strategy, and a system should not automatically give history more authority than a current explicit request. Old preferences may expire: balance responsiveness with stability.

Dynamic profiles, preference drift and context

Dynamic user profile (unlike a static registration profile) takes in new goals, context, evidence: categorise attributes by origin and reliability, use timestamps (old preferences may need reconsideration), separate confirmed facts from uncertain estimates, keep it correctable. Preference drift: interests, requirements or priorities change over time, gradually or through life events; drift detection is essential for reliability and safety. Types: data, concept, prediction.

DriftWhat changes
Data driftDistribution of input features differs from training (user behaviour, collection, seasonality)
Concept driftInput-to-correct-output relationship changes; the learned mapping is obsolete
Prediction driftDistribution of model outputs changes even if inputs stay stable

Responses to detected drift: check data quality, analyse drift, do nothing, retrain, adapt logic, find segments, use fallback, rebuild. Context-aware personalisation adjusts interfaces in real time; limit signals to task relevance, keep adaptation predictable, evaluate context's contribution. Success factors: user first; clear data-usage policies, understandable privacy settings.

Feedback loops, exposure bias, diversity and exploration

The personalisation feedback loop links recommendations to later interactions; feedback (explicit or implicit) matters only if processed appropriately. Exposure bias: clicks are conditional on what the recommender showed.

Diversity: D@K = distinct categories in the top K ÷ K. List A: five business-analytics courses, Diversity@5 = 0.20 (1 of 5); List B: 0.60 (3 of 5; the picture tags five categories, quote 0.60); both stay relevant (95% down to 90% in A, to 86% in B). Benefit: users discover complementary skills.

Exploration vs exploitation: exploitation recommends items currently estimated highly relevant; exploration adds options that teach the system about the user. Too much exploitation reinforces narrow recommendations; too much exploration reduces immediate usefulness. Weigh user context and exploration cost.

User control, feedback and boundaries

Recommendations support decisions, not replace them. A visible settings menu is not control: controls must change behaviour, let users correct assumptions, and show whether a change took effect. The level depends on the task's consequences.

Feedback must be specific, acknowledged without overstating its effect, not burdensome, and judged by whether it improves the next meaningful interaction:

User feedbackAppropriate response

"Wrong prerequisite information"Flag it for verification

Respecting boundaries: separate hard constraints from ranking preferences, allow opt-out, collect data in proportion to purpose, explain boundaries accessibly; protects brand integrity and revenue.

Why adaptive AI needs new UX research approaches

  • The mechanism matters. Evaluate the mechanism, not only the interface (updates change the experience, not the layout). Technical fluency separates algorithmic from interaction-design failures, without building models.
  • Non-deterministic AI may differ under identical conditions. Separate variation from incorrectness, decide what must stay stable, repeat trials, document model version, prompt, context, retrieval snapshot, tool responses, decoding configuration, rubric.
  • Sessions, not single responses. Errors arise at different stages; success reflects the real objective. Problems: irrelevance, low quality, high effort, cognitive load, many revisions; solutions: flipped interaction, question refinement, collaborative task definition.
  • A/B testing: two versions to different users (A original, B variation); hypothesis, random assignment, measures set beforehand, effect sizes with uncertainty.
  • Causal inference. Observational comparisons may be confounded; before-and-after does not prove causation; recommenders add selection and exposure biases; randomised experiments are one approach.
  • Longitudinal evaluation follows the same users over time: reveals preference drift, delayed or cumulative effects; evaluate stability and appropriate adaptation.
  • Behavioural telemetry: recorded interactions; shows patterns interviews miss. Define events consistently, avoid unnecessary personal data, privacy shapes the architecture.

Emerging trends and innovations in UX research

  • Human evaluation of generative AI. Accuracy and helpfulness differ; rubrics need observable criteria; evaluators can be biased. Slide rubric (1, 3, 5): accuracy, relevance, clarity, actionability.
  • AI-assisted qualitative analysis: grounded themes, human review, traceability, audit trail, verification effort counted. NN/g survey of 521 UX practitioners (23 October 2026): 54% cited output quality, 50% privacy or security; convenience sample.
  • Synthetic users are not direct empirical evidence; they may underrepresent diversity. Complement, never replace, human research.
  • Agentic AI: multi-step work toward a goal; delegation is the central interaction; needs boundaries between assistance and authorisation, and visibility into actions. Measure end-to-end task success separately from step success (nine correct steps then an unauthorised submission is not success), because errors propagate; evaluate recovery; too many confirmations cancel delegation's benefit. Measures: task completion, tool-use correctness, intervention frequency, error severity, user understanding.
  • Generative UI: AI creates interface elements, extending adaptation from content to structure ("same interface for everyone" becomes "personalised for each user"); evaluate as working interactions.

Answers the slides keep giving

  • "Does more X make it better?" No: more modalities, a newer framework, higher accuracy, a more impressive VR scene or one better metric do not by themselves improve the experience. The answer is always "for which user, task and context?".
  • "Which perspective can you ignore?" None (lecture 08, slide 53).
  • "What if the AI is wrong?" Show uncertainty, let the user verify, reject, correct or override, recover to a safe state, and keep consequential decisions with the human.
  • "Is it better?" Judge on several measures together (a scorecard), say which one got worse, and test the change with users or an experiment.

  2. Run the course's eight questions (Start tab): problem, why AI, could plain UX do it, what if wrong, how the user understands it, can they override, what stays human, how to measure.
  3. Close with a measurable indicator: task success, time, errors, satisfaction, trust or override rate, compared before and after.
Sources: the eight lecture decks (402 slides, including the 80 that are mostly pictures, read from the slide images), the Moodle course pages and the five homework briefs, all read on 17 Nov 2026. The practice questions and model answers are written from the slides, not taken from the lecturer's key; answers marked "unsure" rest on a reading of open wording. The value-matrix example is an illustration, not course data.
