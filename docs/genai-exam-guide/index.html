Principles and foundations of AI and GenAI

What AI is · three ways to classify it · how an AI system works · pipelines and RAG
The idea

AI is sorted by intelligence, by functionality and by technology. Every system follows dataset, training algorithm, model, input, output; GenAI is often chained into pipelines.

What AI is

Artificial intelligence is the ability of computer systems to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence.

The six tasks (slide 2)

  1. (Machine) learning
  2. Reasoning
  3. Problem solving
  4. Language recognition and generation
  5. Image recognition
  6. Decision making

Three classifications that follow

  • Level of intelligence: Narrow AI, General AI, Superintelligence (slide 3).
  • Level of functionality: Reactive, Limited Memory, Theory of Mind, Self-aware (slides 5-8).
  • Technological level: Symbolic, Statistical, Neural, Generative (slides 10-11).

More detail

Reactive AI (slide 5)

  • Reacts only to the current situation, using predefined rules or algorithms.
  • Cannot recall past actions and cannot learn from experience.
  • Does not operate on the principle of machine learning.
  • Suitable for simple, well-defined tasks.
  • Example: IBM Deep Blue analysed possible moves but did not remember previous games or strategies.

Limited Memory AI (slide 6)

  • Can temporarily store information and use it for decision-making, so it uses temporarily stored historical data to make decisions.
  • Stores a limited amount of information about past events and uses historical data for decisions.
  • The memory is not long-term or self-updating.
  • Machine learning here typically uses supervised learning.
  • Lacks self-awareness; it cannot reflect on its own actions.
  • Example: autonomous vehicles track other vehicles, road signs, speed and previous events for a short time to decide, for example to brake or turn.

Theory of Mind AI (slide 7)

  • Can understand the thoughts, intentions, emotions and motives of other entities; a step toward social intelligence.
  • Understands that others think differently.
  • Predicts behaviour based on emotions, intentions or context.
  • Adapts to social situations.
  • Can interpret indirect cues, for example sarcasm, irony and hints.

Self-aware AI (slide 8)

  • A theoretical level: the system perceives itself as a separate entity with an internal state, goals and actions.
  • It has a model that describes its own functioning, state and capabilities, and can "think" about itself.
  • It analyses its own decisions and learning and adjusts behaviour from internal understanding, not only external data.
  • It sets its own goals based on its "interests" or strategies.
  • It need not have consciousness like a human, but may know it exists, that it is observed and that its actions have consequences.

Core of AI: how a system works

  1. Dataset: text, images, audio recordings collected to train the AI.
  2. Training algorithm: finds patterns, relationships and rules in the data.
  3. Model: created by training; predicts or generates answers from new data.
  4. Input: a question, a photo, a document.
  5. Output: an answer, a prediction, a recommendation, a classification or another decision.
More detail
  1. Dataset: a large amount of data is collected, such as text, images, audio recordings, used to train the AI.
  2. Training algorithm: the data is fed into it; it analyses the data for patterns, relationships and rules and "learns" to recognise situations and make decisions.
  3. Model: the AI model created by training; it uses the accumulated knowledge to predict or generate answers from new data.
  4. Input: the user gives new information or a query: a question, a photo, a document or other data.
  5. Output: the model processes the input using what it learned; the result is an answer, a prediction, a recommendation, a classification or another decision.

The slide 17 picture shows text, images, audio and video going into a foundational model ("billions of parameters"), and a prompt steering it, with text, images, audio and video coming out. The lecture splits it into four steps:

  1. Large datasets: GenAI models need very large amounts of data to learn patterns. Language models train on billions of text documents; image models on millions of labelled images; audio models on large collections of speech and music recordings.
  2. Model creation: training ends with a model that holds accumulated knowledge about patterns in the data. It does not memorise all the data verbatim; it learns general relationships and rules.
  3. Generating new content: given a user query (prompt), the model uses the learned patterns to generate a new output: text, an image, audio, a video or program code.

Example of a neural network: recognising a dog

  • Digitisation first: an image is a matrix of numbers. A 28×28-pixel grayscale image gives 784 input neurons (28 × 28), one per pixel, each from 0 (black) to 1 (white).
  • The first hidden layers look for simple geometric structures; an L3 neuron activates only on specific combinations of L2 neurons.
  • L4 recognises specific parts (ears, paws, tail, fur texture); L5 understands how the parts relate to each other.
  • Slide labels: L1 pixel values, L2 edges, L3 combinations of edges, L4 features, L5 combinations of features; output "Dog".
More detail
  • The first hidden layers do not respond to the whole image at once. They look for simple geometric structures, like understanding a letter by seeing only single strokes.
  • In L3 a neuron activates only when it gets signals from specific combinations of L2 neurons.
  • The slide picture labels the layers: Layer 1 pixel values detected, L2 edges identified, L3 combinations of edges identified, L4 features identified, L5 combinations of features identified; the output is "Dog".

Pipelines

A pipeline is a sequential chain of models and processes.

Text-to-image example: text prompt, language model, image generation model, style adaptation model, final result. The language model picks out objects, environment and style; an extra model adjusts artistic style, colours or quality.

  • Smaller steps: they allow you to solve complex tasks by breaking them down into smaller steps.
  • Specialisation: each model has a specific task.
  • No giant model: no need to train one massive model from scratch.
  • Flexibility: easier to update, more efficient.
More detail

Modern GenAI tools often use not one model but several connected ones. A pipeline is a sequential chain of models and processes.

Why use pipelines?

  • No giant model: there is no need to build and train a single massive model from scratch.
  • Flexibility: the system becomes more flexible, easier to update, and more efficient.

RAG pipeline

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) combines information retrieval and response generation: instead of relying only on knowledge acquired during training, the LLM first searches a knowledge base.

  1. User query: for example the procedure for defending a university thesis.
  2. Information retrieval: searches a document repository or knowledge base.
  3. Contextual enrichment: the found information is linked to the question, creating an expanded query (the slide picture calls it "Augmented": query plus data).
  4. Generation: the LLM answers from the question plus the retrieved information.
  5. Result: an accurate, relevant answer.
TrapStyle adaptation belongs to the text-to-image pipeline, not to RAG. The RAG stages are information retrieval, contextual enrichment and generation.
More detail
  1. Information retrieval: the system searches a document repository or knowledge base and finds relevant documents, rules or procedures.
  2. Contextual enrichment: the found information is linked to the user's question, creating an expanded query: the question "What is the defence procedure?" plus extracts from the university regulations. The slide picture calls this step "Augmented", with a prompt of query plus data.
  3. Generation: the large language model receives the question together with the retrieved information and generates a response based on that context.

Prompts, tokens and the context window

Prompt: an instruction or context that guides the model's output; its structure, clarity and precision decide the answer's quality. Models generate text step by step from probabilistic predictions (s3).

TrapThe window is input plus output, not just the prompt: 4,096 − 1,000 = 3,096 tokens left. Tokens are also what services charge for.
More detail
  • Token: the basic unit of an LLM: a word (“cat”), a partial word (“ca” + “t”) or a symbol (“@”, “#”, “!”). Spaces and punctuation marks can also be separate tokens.
  • An LLM predicts the next token from the previous ones, so it “thinks” in token sequences, not in words or sentences. Most commercial LLM services charge by tokens consumed, so optimising token use cuts cost.
  • Context window: the maximum number of tokens an LLM can process during a single interaction: the input (prompt) plus the output it generates.
  • Example (lecturer): A 1,000-token prompt in a 4,096-token window leaves 3,096 tokens for the response. If the response exceeds that, it is truncated.
  • Past the limit, older information is discarded (the model “forgets” it), so the user must plan which information to put in the prompt.
TrapThe window is input plus output, not just the prompt. A question that gives a window size and a prompt length asks for the subtraction: 4,096 − 1,000 = 3,096.

Hallucination

Hallucination: convincing but wrong or invented information: non-existent dates, sources, technologies or quotes (notes, s6).

How to reduce the risk (slide 6)

  • Ask for sources.
  • Ask about the confidence level.
  • Provide clear context.
  • Verify important information on your own: especially scientific, legal or medical information.

Causes (notes, s6)

  • Knowledge gaps: the model lacks evidence and fills in with statistical guesses.
  • Overly confident tone: false reliability.
  • Insufficient context: picks the most likely, not necessarily correct, option.

Slide 7 (arXiv 2509.04664, 4 Sep 2025): models hallucinate because they are encouraged to: evaluation metrics award higher scores to models that give partially incorrect information than to models that admit “I don’t know” (like guessing on an exam).

TrapSlide-6 notes give causes; slide 7 the incentive. Trusting a confident answer is the opposite of the advice.
More detail

Slide 7, “Why Language Models Hallucinate” (arXiv 2509.04664, 4 Sep 2025): models hallucinate because they are encouraged to. Evaluation metrics award higher scores to models that give partially incorrect information than to models that admit “I don’t know”, so it pays more to guess than to be honest. Exam analogy: when you do not know the answer, it is often better to guess than to leave the question blank, and models behave the same way because the system rewards them for it.

TrapTwo different explanations. The slide-6 notes give causes (knowledge gaps, confident tone, missing context); slide 7 gives the incentive (scoring rewards guessing over “I don’t know”). “Trust a confident answer on a scientific, legal or medical topic” is the opposite of the advice.

General LLM architecture and the attention mechanism

  • Idea (slide 8): every token "attends" to every other token, weighting importance for context.
  • Q, K, V come from the embeddings via learned weight matrices. Steps (slide 9): QKᵀ similarity, softmax weights, weighted sum of V.
  • Runs in parallel on GPUs/TPUs; memory bandwidth and tensor cores are critical.
  • Example (slide 10): Artificial little, intelligence great, is little, education great: Artificial intelligence → transforms → education.
More detail

Slide 7 draws one attention head. The input embeddings X (example tokens "I", "love", "tennis") are multiplied by learned weight matrices to give Q, K and V.

  • For each input sequence three matrices are computed: Query (Q), Key (K), Value (V). They are derived from the embeddings via learned weight matrices.
  • Step 1 (slide 9): the dot product QKᵀ measures similarity between words.
  • Step 2: the softmax normalises these scores into attention weights.
  • Step 3: the weighted sum of V gives the contextualised word representation.
  • These are matrix multiplications executed in parallel on GPUs/TPUs; high memory bandwidth and optimised tensor cores are critical for efficiency.

Worked example (slide 10). For "Artificial intelligence is transforming education." the importance of each word is: Artificial = little, intelligence = great, is = little, education = great. The model therefore reads the core meaning as Artificial intelligence → transforms → education.

Diffusion models and GANs

Diffusion models generate data (such as images or sound) by starting with completely random noise and gradually transforming it into a consistent output through a series of denoising steps, each reversing the noise added to real data in training.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) generate new, realistic-looking data with two competing neural networks that learn together: the Generator (G) and the Discriminator (D).

Repeat: the better the generator, the harder for the discriminator, and the reverse.

Where the two models appearThe video slide on model types (35) names diffusion models, transformers and autoencoders and does not name GANs.
More detail
  • Discriminator step: it gets real data and the generator's fake data. If it classifies correctly (e.g. says the generator's data is fake), its weights are not adjusted or only minimally; if it is wrong, they are adjusted so it recognises data more accurately next time.
  • Generator step: it creates fake data and passes it to the discriminator. The generator's parameters are adjusted so that the discriminator's decision is as close as possible to REAL. In other words, it learns to make better forgeries that fool the discriminator.

Multimodal generative AI

Text, image, audio and video in one system · how it works · how to design, prompt, synchronise and evaluate
The idea

Multimodal generative AI handles several data types (text, images, audio, video) in one system: each modality is encoded into a shared vector space, an LLM core processes it, and diffusion models create the media. The craft is keeping outputs consistent, coherent and in sync.

Why multimodal AI matters

Four reasons (slide 2), in order:

  1. It combines language, vision, sound and motion for richer storytelling.
  2. It supports immersive design for games, XR, installations and performances.
  3. It allows interactive and adaptive experiences where multiple outputs respond to a single input.
  4. It bridges gaps between composition, visuals, narrative and audiovisual experience.

Modality: unimodal versus multimodal AI

Modality: a type of data input, such as text, images, audio or video.

  • Unimodal: traditional AI systems are unimodal, meaning they process only one data type at a time.
  • Multimodal: combines modalities: inputs from several sources (e.g. text and images), outputs reflecting this diverse data.
More detail

Modality: in the context of AI, a modality is a type of data input. It can be any form of data, such as text, images, audio or video.

  • Unimodal: traditional AI systems are unimodal, meaning they process only one data type at a time. Example: a language model like the early versions of ChatGPT could only handle text inputs, so it could only give text-based responses.
  • Multimodal: breaks these boundaries by combining different modalities. The system receives inputs from multiple sources, such as text and images, and generates outputs that reflect this diverse data. This makes AI more versatile and better informed.

How multimodal models work: the four ideas

  1. Unified Representation: data from different media is transformed into a common vector space (embeddings).
  2. Cross-Modal Influence: one modality (e.g. text) guides the generation of others (image, audio, video).
  3. Sequential or Parallel Generation: Sequential generates one modality first, then others; Parallel generates all simultaneously from a single input.
  4. Consistency & Coherence: outputs across modalities match story, mood and style.

The rule to memorise: Sequential Generation prioritizes quality and control, while Parallel Generation focuses on speed and efficiency. Modern models often combine both, task by task.

More detail
  1. Unified Representation: data from different media is transformed into a common vector space (embeddings). AI learns joint embeddings for the different media types, so the model can understand the relationships between words, images, sounds and other forms of information.

Inside the model: encoder, projection, LLM core, diffusion

  • Text: tokens fed directly to the LLM; text output is generated directly by it.
  • Encoder (image, audio, video): each data type has its own, extracting feature vectors. Examples of encoders: Vision Transformer for images and Whisper for sound.

  • Output projection: converts the vectors into a format for a specific generation model.
  • Diffusion models: generative models (e.g. Stable Diffusion for images, or diffusion models for sound synthesis) that create the final high-quality image, audio track or video.

Waveform models are generative AI models that work directly with an audio signal (raw audio waveform), rather than with notes or MIDI information. They analyze and generate a real sound wave, so they can create music, speech, singing, environmental sounds or sound effects. An audio signal is a sequence of thousands or millions of amplitude values: 0.12, 0.25, -0.10, -0.45, 0.31, ... During training, the AI analyzes: voice timbre, the sound of musical instruments, intonation, sound effects, background sounds, acoustic properties. Later, the model can generate a completely new audio signal.

How AI learns sound

  • Training data: thousands or millions of samples: music (melodies, harmonies, rhythms, instrumentations); speech (voices, accents, emotional tones); environmental sounds (nature, machines, effects).
  • Model types: Autoencoders & Transformers for pattern recognition; Diffusion models for generating realistic waveforms.
  • Output: AI "imagines" new sound sequences from prompts or examples; it does not copy existing sounds.
