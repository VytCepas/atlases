01

UX in AI

The role of user experience in AI solutions
The idea

An AI feature succeeds only when people can use its outputs to achieve meaningful goals, not merely when the model predicts accurately. UX covers expectations before use, interaction during use and the consequences after a recommendation is followed, so the whole socio-technical system is what gets designed.

Why UX matters in AI solutions

UX in AI (slide 21) is the total experience produced when humans interact with systems whose behaviour is partly generated, predicted, inferred or adapted through AI. The question is not only "can the user operate the interface?" but "does the user understand what the system is doing, why, and when its output should be trusted?" (slide 10):

  • Interpretation: the interface can frame an uncertain recommendation as advice or as an apparently authoritative command.
  • Action: users need to verify evidence, edit results, reject suggestions, recover from mistakes or request human assistance.
Accuracy is not experienceTwo apps with the same 95% accuracy can differ completely in UX. The one that explains its recommendations and lets users reject wrong predictions wins on interpretation, action and recovery, even with a plainer interface (slide 3 warm-up).

HCI and its history

HCI (slide 11): the human and the computer determine each other's behaviour over time, framed by the human's goals and tasks. The three parts are the user, the computer or device, and the task. Moore's law (slide 17): transistor counts double every two years; the conclusion is that interfaces become more and more important. Stages (slides 14 to 16): 1980s human factors, ergonomics and cognitive science; 1990s sociology and work psychology, with complex systems as socio-technical systems; 2000s art, philosophy and design, where UX includes aesthetics and affect.

User experience, the 4Cs and ISO 9241-210

User experience (slide 20): the overall experience a user has with a product, system or service. Don Norman coined the term (slide 22): no product is an island. UX designs must consider the context of use (slide 30): training, conventions, laws, competing products, human abilities and limitations.

C3 is "Context" but talks about contentThe slide explains Context through content (text, images, videos, clickable elements). Do not confuse it with the "context of use" on slide 30.
Not just "was it usable"The ISO wording covers anticipated use and the time before and after use, and it includes emotions, beliefs and comfort. A definition that stops at "easy to use" is incomplete.

UX vs UI, and the myths about good design

UXUI
Scopethe user's entire interaction with the product, service or companythe visual and interactive elements: buttons, icons, layouts, navigation

Four myths about good design (slides 33 and 35): good design is just cool graphics; just common sense; the marketing department takes care of understanding customer needs; we can fix the interface at the end.

Human-AI collaboration and human-centered AI

HAIC (slides 37 and 39): humans and AI working together, each leveraging their strengths. Humans: judgment, creativity, leadership, strategy. AI: data analysis, pattern recognition, processing speed, scalability. A HAIC task has goals, interaction and task allocation (slide 38). HCAI (slide 41): AI that amplifies and augments rather than displaces human abilities, preserves human control and operates transparently, with equitable outcomes and respect for privacy. Its three largest attributes (slide 46): reliable, safe, trustworthy.

Control and automation are not a trade-offThe grid's two axes are independent. The goal is the top-right corner, not a point on the line between human control and automation.
Questions the lecture asksUX in AI
  • Warm-up (slide 3): two AI apps both have 95% accuracy. A has an attractive interface but no explanation or correction option; B is simpler but explains recommendations and lets users reject incorrect predictions. Which gives a better UX, and why?
    Model answer: B. Accuracy is equal, so the difference is interpretation and action (verify, reject, recover); A can push users to accept a wrong output with no way back.
  • End-of-discussion question (slide 50): a university AI course recommender sometimes overlooks prerequisites, and students can accept recommendations immediately with no explanation or warning. How would you redesign it for transparency, decision quality, user control and overall UX?
    Model answer: Show why each course is recommended; check prerequisites and warn when one is missing; let students see the evidence, edit, reject and ask a human adviser; remove one-click acceptance and measure decision quality and safe reliance (slide 4).

AI architecture

Architecture for scalable and user-centered AI systems
The idea

An AI system is several cooperating components, not a model bolted to an interface, and the way they are organised decides the experience: how fast answers arrive, how reliable the service is. User expectations such as fast, reliable and trustworthy must be turned into measurable architectural requirements.

Why architecture determines the experience of AI

An accurate model can still give an unsatisfactory experience if predictions arrive too late to support meaningful decisions (slide 5). AI capability concerns predict, classify, generate, recommend; AI usability concerns understand, complete tasks, recover from mistakes, exercise appropriate control (slide 11).

Capability is not usabilityA system can predict well and still fail users who cannot understand it, finish their task, recover from its mistakes or control it appropriately.

From UX principles to architectural requirements

The UX principles are usability, accessibility, responsiveness, transparency and trust (slide 10). Architectural decisions should turn abstract UX expectations into measurable system capabilities.

Three or four?Slide 15 says the needs "can be grouped into three categories", but slide 16 lists four: functional, nonfunctional, operational and responsible-AI. Learn all four.

Architectural decision process (slide 20): requirement, alternative designs, benefits and limitations, selection, evaluation. Select patterns by actual requirements, not technological popularity. Do not maximise every metric: more capacity may cut response times but raises cost and energy use (slide 21).

Architectural qualities

Five qualities (slides 22 to 25): scalability (support growing workloads), elasticity (adjust resources as demand changes), reliability (deliver intended functionality consistently), availability (operational when needed), adaptability (accommodate new models and requirements).

The five layers of a user-centered AI system

Application layer (slide 29): coordinates communication between interface, data, models and external systems. An API sends requests and receives responses; orchestration sets the order in which services run. A request goes user, interface, API, retrieval service, AI model, response (slide 28). Treat training and inference as distinct workloads (slide 30). In RAG (slide 33) the framework fetches context first, then prompts the LLM: question, semantic search in the vector database, contextual data back, prompt to the LLM, post processing, response.

Scaling, reliability and deployment

Identify the limiting component before choosing a scaling strategy (slide 35). Vertical is simpler but less fault-tolerant; horizontal is more cost-effective and fault-tolerant, but more complex. Reliability techniques (slide 36): redundancy, health checks, controlled retries, failover, service isolation.

Monolith: one unit, deployed together. Modular monolith: clear internal boundaries, one deployment. Microservices: independently deployable, so inference scales independently (slide 38). Cloud inference: centralised resources. Edge: can improve offline operation and reduce network-dependent latency. Hybrid: both. The choice depends on latency, privacy, connectivity, device capability and cost (slide 40).

Evaluating the architecture for UX

A successful AI architecture must satisfy both technical and user-experience metrics (slide 42):

Technical evaluationUser-centered evaluation
response time and latencyperceived responsiveness
system availabilityability to access services reliably
request throughputconsistent performance during high demand

Technologies and frameworks for web front-end development

Front-end tech · what runs in the browser, and what it does to the user's experience
The idea

The front end is what the user sees and touches. It is built from HTML (content), CSS (presentation) and JavaScript (behaviour), and frameworks, rendering strategies and performance measures sit on top. New technology appears when user needs outgrow the old one, but a newer technology does not automatically give a better experience: it must be judged against complexity and the real requirements.

How a web application works

The front end is the browser side; the back end is the server side. They talk by HTTP: 200 OK success, 400 Bad Request a client-side problem, 500 Internal Server Error a server failure.

The browser is not a trusted placeUsers can inspect and modify the code and requests in their own browser. So sensitive credentials, authorisation decisions and official eligibility checks must be enforced by protected backend services.

Accessibility, HTML, CSS, JavaScript, forms

  • POUR (WCAG): Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust.
  • HTML gives content its meaning and structure; CSS controls how elements appear; JavaScript reacts to user actions and updates the page through the DOM.
  • Forms: validation only in the browser is insufficient for security. An error message identifies the problem and explains how to correct it; keep the correctly entered values.
  • Imperative UI describes the sequence of operations that change the interface (manual updates, direct DOM manipulation). Declarative UI describes the interface for a given state, with automatic reconciliation (React, Angular, Vue).

Why web technologies evolve

A static website loads fast, navigates predictably and suits users who want stable information; JavaScript adds dynamic behaviour at the cost of complexity and chances for error.

Frameworks, shared state and Single-Page Applications

Modern rendering and Progressive Web Apps

Measuring real UX: LCP, INP and CLS

Do not mix up the threeLCP = largest element rendered (loading). INP = feedback after an interaction (responsiveness). CLS = unexpected movement (stability).

Security, privacy and streaming AI output

  • Secret API keys must not be embedded in client-side JavaScript (slide 47).
  • Rendering untrusted information as executable HTML can introduce Cross-Site Scripting (XSS); AI-generated text is untrusted data, so use text-rendering.
  • Streamed text must not be confused with a finalised or verified result (slide 48). Streams can stop: indicate incomplete output and give retry or continuation options.

User-centred design principles

User-centred design (UCD) · research the users first, then design, test and repeat
The idea

User-centred design lets the users' characteristics, needs, goals, tasks and contexts guide every design decision, so that systems help people reach meaningful goals effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily, not just deliver technically correct functions. It runs as a cycle: understand the context of use, specify requirements, produce designs, evaluate them with users, and iterate.

What UCD is, and why AI needs it

AI predictions must be presented through interfaces that users can understand, evaluate and control. Decisions should come from evidence (observations, interviews, usability evaluations), not only from developers' assumptions.

ISO 9241-210:2019 and the five major UCD principles

Five or six?The ISO standard has six principles; the blog graphic (slide 5) has five. When a question says "ISO 9241-210" use the six. The slide 5 image prints "interactive", a wording slip for iterative.

The human-centred design process: a continuous cycle

Stage 1 investigates users, tasks, environments, practices and constraints. Stage 2 turns research findings into explicit statements of required capabilities and qualities. Stage 3 produces sketches, wireframes and prototypes; each iteration begins with a clear objective or hypothesis. Stage 4 evaluates with representative users on realistic tasks.

User research, interviews and triangulation

  • Needfinding: who are the users, how do they do it now, in what context? Do not think of "generic" users: split the categories.
  • Interviews: semi-structured, open-ended, avoiding leading and biased questions. Poor: "Would an AI chatbot make course selection easier?" Better: "How do you currently select your courses?"
  • Contextual inquiry studies how users do tasks in their actual environment, revealing workarounds and interruptions.
  • Triangulation: interviews show how users interpret, observation shows actual behaviour, task measurements quantify. Findings that disagree across methods must be investigated.

Personas, empathy maps and journey maps

  • A persona has a clear primary goal, built from recurring research patterns; it shows how different users approach the same task.
  • An empathy map organises a user into what they say (explicitly states), think (questions, concerns, beliefs supported by research), do (observable actions) and feel (emotions supported by evidence).
  • A journey map visually shows the sequence of stages, actions, decisions and touchpoints toward a goal, with emotions and difficulties over time.
Do not mix up the three artefactsThe persona is who (a profile with goals). The empathy map is what they say, think, do and feel at one moment. The journey map is the sequence of stages over time with emotions and pain points.

Evaluate with usability testing

Usability is how effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily specified users achieve specified goals in a context of use. Usability testing aims to find problems in the design, not to judge participants' intelligence or technical competence.

  • Conducting: informed consent, tasks as real goals without revealing controls or steps, no leading questions, no hints.
  • Severity depends on frequency, impact on task completion, consequences and difficulty of recovery; fix consequential errors and blockers before minor visual inconsistencies, then re-test.
Efficiency is not satisfactionEfficiency is objective resources (time, effort, number of actions). Satisfaction is the subjective feeling (ease of use, confidence, comfort). Effectiveness is reaching the intended outcome correctly and completely. Slide 50 adds user errors as a fourth thing to measure.

  • Warm-up (slide 3). A course-recommending AI assistant that the team thought intuitive confuses students. What should the team have done before designing and building?   • End-of-class discussion (slide 53). Why is UCD iterative rather than done once? Design thinking

Applying design thinking to AI projects · understand people before you choose the technology
The idea

Design thinking is a human-centred way to solve complex, poorly defined problems. It runs through Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test and Iterate, and it asks first whether AI is needed at all. Model accuracy is not the same as a good user experience, so every AI concept is tested with real tasks and revised from evidence.

From user-centred design to design thinking

UCD gives evidence about users and evaluation of solutions; design thinking gives structure for exploring and refining alternatives to complex, poorly defined problems. They are complementary, and design thinking puts understanding people before deciding features, technologies or architectures. Characteristics: empathy-driven, iterative, collaborative, solution-oriented. IDEO's matrix (slide 3): desirability, viability and feasibility.

The stages of design thinking

The Double Diamond

The Design Council developed it in 2005, built on divergent and convergent thinking. Teams develop customer personas and journey maps in Develop.

Do not mix up the two modelsThe six design-thinking stages (Empathize to Iterate) and the four Double Diamond phases (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver) are different frameworks. "Define" appears in both, but in the Double Diamond it closes the problem diamond.

Why design thinking matters in AI projects

  • Model performance and UX are different dimensions of success. Accuracy, precision and recall describe model behaviour; UX evaluation asks whether users accomplish meaningful tasks effectively.
  • Design thinking asks whether AI is appropriate at all.
Examiner trap"High accuracy means a good AI product" is false on these slides. Accuracy measures the model; UX measures whether users complete real tasks well.

Empathize and Define

  • Separate user goals from requested features. One working professional's problem is a lead, not a rule: 9 of 12 learners reporting trouble is a descriptive result, not evidence that 75% of all working learners have it.
  • Users expect of AI (slide 18): trust, privacy, transparency, control.
  • An empathy map (says, thinks, does, feels) turns the persona into insights and design opportunities.
One persona is not a populationChelsea is a persona, an example user. Assuming that all international students share her needs is not appropriate: it needs further research before generalising.

A good problem definition describes a meaningful user difficulty, is specific enough to guide decisions, stays open to different solutions, and covers who, what, and why (slide 23 also asks where). A POV statement is a user + an important need + a research-informed insight; an HMW question then opens solutions. In HMW, How stresses possible approaches, Might invites experiment and admits uncertainty, We stresses collaboration.

Solution-first framing"We must build an LLM" restricts creativity: simpler, cheaper solutions get overlooked.

Ideate, Prototype, Test, Iterate

  • AI is one possible solution, not the default: rule-based systems fit clear conditions; AI can add value when relationships are hard to encode manually. Brainwriting: ideas contributed independently before group discussion. Crazy Eights: eight ideas in eight minutes.
  • A prototype's purpose is learning; it does not always need a trained model. Wizard-of-Oz simulates an intelligent system through human-generated responses. Include normal and problematic scenarios.
  • Test trust against actual system reliability. Prioritise problems by consequence.
Top-ranked is not automatically correctA correct selection meets all mandatory requirements, so a top-ranked course that breaks one does not count.

  • Is it appropriate to assume all international students have Chelsea's needs? (slide 19) No. A persona is one example user.
  • Which single problem would you investigate first, and what evidence would justify that priority? (slide 21) One need, e.g. schedule compatibility, justified by how many users, how often, how severe.
  • Exercise: three ways to improve Chelsea's course selection without AI and three with AI. (slide 27) Without AI: prerequisite checks, excluding unavailable time slots, a comparison table. With AI: goal-matched recommendations, explanations.

  • Should the highest-ranked AI recommendation automatically count as the correct selection? (slide 36) No. It must satisfy all mandatory requirements.
  • self-assessment Accurate recommendations, but users choose courses that conflict with their schedules. Which stage do you revisit first, and why? Multimodal UX

Multimodal UX design · voice, touch and gesture, chosen and coordinated
The idea

Multimodal UX is not about adding more ways to interact. It is about selecting, combining and coordinating voice, touch, gesture and feedback so users can work with an AI system naturally, reliably and accessibly. Each modality has its own strengths and failure modes, so the design must handle context, conflicts, fallbacks, devices and accessibility.

What multimodal design is

  • Three components: input, output and context. Success needs meaningful coordination: preserve context, give consistent feedback, and let users continue when one modality becomes unavailable.
Warm-up answerOffering voice, touch and gesture does not automatically make the AI Course Planner easier to use. More modalities do not automatically give better UX: the benefit comes from the appropriate interaction being available for the task and context.

Voice, touch and contactless gesture

Main design concern: voice, speech errors and dialogue; touch, target accuracy and discoverability; contactless gesture, recognition reliability and fatigue. Hands-free is possible by voice and gesture, not touch.

Voice-based UX

A VUI supports hands-free and eyes-free activity; it is weak for presenting large amounts of detail and adds uncertainty in speech recognition and language interpretation.

  • Recognition errors: the transcript differs from what was said. Interpretation errors can occur even when the transcript is right. Distinguish uncertainty from failure: clarify when ambiguous; repeated failures must change strategy. Evaluate with both transcription and task measures.
Word Error Rate (standard ASR metric)WER = (S + D + I) / N × 100%S = substitutions · D = deletions · I = insertions · N = words in the reference transcript

Touch-based UX and gesture

  • Do not require users to discover an invisible swipe for essential content. Gesture meanings should follow platform conventions.
  • Target size: Android recommends at least 48 x 48 dp; Apple 44 x 44 px (slide 24). Do not mix this up with WCAG 2.2 Level AA's 24 x 24 CSS pixel minimum criterion.
  • Fitts' Law: MT = a + b · log2(1 + D / W); a farther (D) or smaller (W) target means longer movement time.
  • A secret swipe-left delete becomes a visible Remove action with Undo; small side-by-side buttons become larger, separated controls (slide 27).
  • Touch gestures need contact; contactless gestures are seen by cameras or other motion sensors. Challenges: sensor limits, lighting, fatigue, unintentional gestures. Keep alternatives.

Choosing the right modality

There is no best modalityThe correct choice depends on the interaction objective and the operating context. An answer that names one "best" modality for everything is wrong on the slides.

Interaction patterns and input fusion

Sequential: different modalities at different stages. Simultaneous: at about the same time. Inputs are complementary (each gives a different piece of one action), redundant (similar information through more than one modality) or independent. Multimodal fusion combines speech, touch and gesture to determine intent.

Context, conflicts, switching and fallback

  • If the student says "Select Course A" while tapping Course B, the system may prioritise deliberate direct selection, but must not automatically submit an enrolment; if unsure, show the alternatives.
  • Modality switching must preserve relevant context; fallback offers an alternative without forcing repetition. Consistency does not require identical layouts: task concepts keep their meaning and application state is preserved.

Accessibility, privacy and evaluation

  • Essential functions must not depend only on speech, and contactless gesture must not be the only way to complete essential tasks (tremors, fatigue); accessibility must hold when switching modalities.
  • Evaluate task completion across modalities, recognition performance (WER for speech; precision, recall for gesture), interaction efficiency including recovery time, and modality switching, conflict resolution and fallback effectiveness.
Self-assessment answerThree input modalities do not automatically make an application accessible: it needs equivalent alternatives for essential functions, accessible feedback, and preserved context when users switch.
Questions the lecture asksMultimodal UX
  • self-assessment Warm-up (slide 4): would offering voice, touchscreen and gesture automatically make the AI Course Planner easier to use? No. The benefit comes from the appropriate interaction for the task and context (slides 5, 9).
  • self-assessment Does providing three input modalities automatically make an application accessible? (slide 45) No. Essential functions need alternatives to speech-only or gesture-only, and context must survive modality switches.
  • End-of-class discussion (slide 48): voice search on a phone, comparison by touch on a tablet, and the assistant selects the wrong course. How would you design for seamless cross-device interaction while preventing and recovering from input errors? Keep task state and core actions consistent across devices (slide 41); clarify uncertain speech and never auto-submit on conflicting input (slides 16, 39); support undo and correction (slides 26, 39); offer touch or text fallback without repeating the request (slide 40); keep accessible alternatives (slides 41, 45).

AR & VR

Augmented and virtual reality · designing for space, body and comfort
The idea

AR adds digital content to the real world, VR replaces most of it with a simulation, and MR stresses how tightly the two interact. Good immersive UX is not realistic graphics: it is meaningful tasks, reliable spatial alignment, comfort, safety and user control. AI helps by recognising objects and guiding users, but the human must stay in control.

AR, VR, MR and XR: what each one is

AR enhances perception of the real world with computer-generated content; VR creates a simulated environment users can explore; XR is the umbrella term for technologies combining or simulating physical and digital experiences (commonly AR, VR and MR; boundaries vary). AR's main UX challenge is aligning content with reality; VR's is maintaining immersion and comfort.

Do not mix them upA headset showing a completely simulated laboratory is VR, not AR or MR: the simulation replaces the direct view. MR is defined by spatial integration of physical and digital elements.

Three functions of AR: visualisation, instruction and interaction (virtual control panels).

Most impressive is not most usefulThe most visually impressive experience is not necessarily the most useful, especially for consequential tasks.

Tracking and performance

3DoF tracks rotation only (pitch, yaw, roll); 6DoF adds position on X, Y and Z, using an IMU with cameras. Sensor fusion merges several sensors into one more accurate estimate; SLAM maps the environment while tracking the system's own position. Latency, the delay between action and response, is detrimental to VR.

UX design for AR

A spatial anchor keeps a label with its physical target; registration aligns virtual objects with physical coordinates in real time; occlusion is the visual depth relation of real and virtual objects; focus+context keeps the relevant scene sharp and the rest blurred. Keep AI-generated guidance separate from authoritative procedural requirements. Never obstruct hazards.

UX design for VR and spatial interaction

Use landmarks for orientation. Spatial interaction can be gaze-based, hand tracking, motion controllers or body movement; choose by the spatial task. Ray casting selects objects beyond reach; direct manipulation suits nearby ones.

Presence vs immersionPresence is the user's state (psychological); immersion is the technology's capability (senses, resolution, richness). An examiner may swap them.

Human-AI collaboration and human control

The goal is effective cooperation, not maximum automation. Recognition and generated guidance can be wrong, so separate verified instructions from uncertain AI suggestions: with low recognition confidence, ask the technician to verify the component first. Human control means confirmation before significant actions, reliable interruption and recovery to a safe state.

Accessibility, comfort and privacy

Navigation settings should be adjustable. Cybersickness arises from a mismatch between the visual, vestibular and proprioceptive senses (dizziness, nausea, disorientation). Devices can collect head poses, hand positions and activity logs.

Evaluating AR/VR user experience

Four measures: task effectiveness, spatial accuracy, presence and comfort.

One metric is not a verdictHigher task success with more reported discomfort does not prove a better experience. Judge across effectiveness, accuracy, presence and comfort together.

Personalisation & trends

Balancing personalisation with user preferences · and how UX research is changing
The idea

An adaptive AI system tailors the experience to each person, so the designer now governs the behaviour of a changing experience instead of drawing fixed screens. Personalisation must be balanced with user-defined constraints, control, privacy and variety, and it must be evaluated with scorecards, experiments and new research methods. Success is measured by what people can meaningfully and responsibly achieve, not by one impressive metric.

Personalisation vs customisation: who is in control?

Personalisation tailors experience and recommendations to an individual without manual input: proactive and data-driven. Customisation puts control in the user's hands: they manually adjust settings or layout, which feels safer and builds ownership. Practical systems are hybrids.

The personalisation loopPersonalisation is self-reinforcing: by serving content that matches past behaviour it can trap users in a loop, reducing exposure to new ideas and limiting discovery.

Explicit and implicit preferences, user models and recommenders

Explicit data is declared: clear, but possibly incomplete, biased or outdated. Implicit data is behavioural: realistic, but ambiguous and a privacy concern. Recommenders are content-based, collaborative (similar users) or hybrid.

Cold start is having no prior data: user cold start (new user), item cold start (new item) and system cold start (new platform, no data at all). A system should not automatically give history more authority than a current explicit request.

Hard constraints, soft preferences and ranking

Mandatory requirements (online, at most 6 hours a week) filter before ranking; desirable ones contribute to ranking; uncertain ones are never turned into a hard rule. Multi-criteria ranking normalises criteria and combines them with weights (S = Σ w·r, slide 25), scoring only after the eligibility check.

Dynamic profiles, drift and feedback loops

Preference drift is when a user's interests change over time. Three types of model drift: data drift (input distribution differs from training), concept drift (the input-output relationship changes) and prediction drift (the output distribution changes).

Exposure bias: the recommender decides what is seen, so clicks depend on exposure: compare click-through rate (clicks ÷ impressions), not raw clicks (slide 24). Diversity: D@K = distinct categories in the top K ÷ K. Exploitation recommends what is estimated relevant; exploration introduces options that teach the system about the user. Too much exploitation narrows recommendations; too much exploration reduces immediate usefulness (slide 27).

User control and boundaries

A visible settings menu is not control: controls must meaningfully influence behaviour and show whether a change took effect. Allow opt-out of optional personalisation.

Evaluation and new research approaches

A scorecard combines recommendation quality (Precision@K, NDCG, diversity), task-level measures, user understanding, and control and safety. Precision@K measures relevance in the first K items; NDCG also weights positions. Technical metrics do not capture user effort.

Adaptive AI needs new methods: evaluate the mechanism, not just the interface; judge whole sessions. Before-and-after comparisons do not prove causation; randomised experiments are one important approach, and A/B testing (A the original, B the variation) is a quantitative method for comparing two versions. Synthetic users are not empirical evidence and never replace human research. Agentic AI needs clear boundaries between assistance and authorisation; measure end-to-end task success separately from step success.

One AI experience: the whole course

  • Success is not what the technology can do, but what people can meaningfully and responsibly achieve with it.
  • Design technology around human needs, not human behaviour around technology.

Homework frameworks

Defined in the homework briefs, not the slides; the exam may assume them
Why this tab is hereThe homework briefs bring in frameworks the slides never define: automation vs augmentation, HITL vs HOTL, critical interaction points, the AI value matrix and the UI matrix. The exam may assume you know them from the practical work. Definitions marked brief come from the Moodle homework pages; those marked general are standard definitions the briefs use without defining.

Does the problem need AI at all?

Exam angle"Which of these problems needs AI?" A missing back button, a confusing form or inconsistent icons are UI fixes. AI earns its place only where the task needs prediction, recognition, recommendation or language, and plain UX cannot do it as well (the course's own principle).

Who stays in control: HITL, HOTL and critical points

  • Automation vs augmentation brief: which activities AI should perform automatically, and which it should support rather than replace human decision-making. Lecture 07 defines augmentation: a machine "amplifies the user's own capabilities".
  • HITL vs HOTL general: in the loop = the human approves each consequential output; on the loop = the AI acts and the human supervises with the power to stop or override.

The AI value matrix and the UI matrix

  • UI matrix brief: for the same four outcomes, how the application should communicate each one to users, e.g. a confident alert for a likely TP, a softer "possible, please check" for uncertain cases, an easy "this was wrong" for FPs, and a visible fallback when the AI may have missed something.

Pairs that get swapped

  • UX (the whole experience) vs UI (the interface you touch)
  • AI capability (what the model can do) vs AI usability (what users can do with it)
  • accuracy (the model) vs UX (whether users finish real tasks well)
  • persona (who) vs empathy map (says, thinks, does, feels) vs journey map (stages over time)
  • efficiency (objective effort) vs satisfaction (subjective feeling)
  • design thinking stages vs double-diamond phases ("Define" is in both)
  • POV statement (user + need + insight) vs HMW question (opens solutions)
  • early fusion (combine inputs) vs late fusion (combine recognisers' decisions)
  • AR (adds to the real world) vs VR (replaces it) vs MR (integrates both)
  • presence (the user's state) vs immersion (the technology's capability)
  • 3DoF (rotation) vs 6DoF (rotation + position)
  • augmentation (amplifies the human) vs automation (replaces the human)
  • personalisation (system-driven) vs customisation (user-driven)
  • explicit (declared) vs implicit (behavioural) preferences
  • hard constraints (filter first) vs soft preferences (rank after)

Answers the slides keep giving

  • "Does more X make it better?" No: more modalities, a newer framework, higher accuracy, a more impressive VR scene or one better metric do not by themselves improve the experience. The answer is always "for which user, task and context?".
  • "Which perspective can you ignore?" None (lecture 08, slide 53).
  • "What if the AI is wrong?" Show uncertainty, let the user verify, reject, correct or override, recover to a safe state, and keep consequential decisions with the human.
  • "Is it better?" Judge on several measures together (a scorecard), say which one got worse, and test the change with users or an experiment.

Three moves for a written question

  1. Name the user, task and context first. Every lecture's answer turns on them.
  2. Run the course's eight questions (Start tab): problem, why AI, could plain UX do it, what if wrong, how the user understands it, can they override, what stays human, how to measure.
  3. Close with a measurable indicator: task success, time, errors, satisfaction, trust or override rate, compared before and after.
