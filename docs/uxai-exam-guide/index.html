UX in AI

The role of user experience in AI solutions
The idea

An AI feature succeeds only when people can use its outputs to achieve meaningful goals, not merely when the model predicts accurately. UX covers expectations before use, interaction during use and the consequences after a recommendation is followed, so the whole socio-technical system is what gets designed. The lecture builds from interfaces, HCI and UX basics up to human-AI collaboration and human-centered AI.

Why UX matters in AI solutions

Slide 4: the role of UX in AI

  • Core question: an AI feature succeeds only when people can use its outputs to achieve meaningful goals, not merely when a model predicts accurately.
  • Human-centred scope: expectations before use, interaction during use, consequences after a recommendation is followed.
  • System boundary: users, tasks, algorithms, interfaces, institutional procedures and the wider social context as one socio-technical system.
  • AI-specific difficulty: uncertain and sometimes plausible-but-wrong outputs create new interpretation, oversight and error-recovery demands.
  • Course perspective: judge AI through effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, decision quality, accessibility and safe reliance.
  • Opening challenge: a beautifully designed dashboard may fail if it encourages users to accept an inappropriate recommendation.

Slide 10: why UX matters in AI-enabled systems

  • Adoption is behavioural: even useful AI delivers no value if intended users cannot discover, understand or confidently operate it.
  • Interpretation matters: the interface can frame an uncertain recommendation as advice or as an apparently authoritative command.
  • Action matters: users need to verify evidence, edit results, reject suggestions, recover from mistakes or request human assistance.
  • Consequences matter: a wrong music recommendation wastes attention; a wrong clinical or academic eligibility recommendation can cause material harm.
  • Context matters: the same output may help a trained advisor and be incomprehensible to a first-year student.
  • Success requires evidence: measure whether the design improves the actual task while preserving user agency, fairness and appropriate reliance.

UX in AI (slide 21) is the total experience produced when humans interact with systems whose behaviour is partly generated, predicted, inferred or adapted through AI. The question is no longer only "can the user operate the interface?" but also "does the user understand what the system is doing, why, and when its output should be trusted?" It connects HCI, UX design, psychology, machine learning, data science, ethics, accessibility, service design and product strategy.

Accuracy is not experienceTwo apps with the same 95% accuracy can differ completely in UX. The one that explains its recommendations and lets users reject wrong predictions wins on interpretation, action and recovery, even with a plainer interface (slide 3 warm-up).

Interfaces, bad UX and Gestalt

Interface (slide 5): a point of interaction between two systems or components that lets them communicate and exchange information; where two systems meet, for example humans and technology. Why it matters (slide 7): interfaces let different software, hardware and systems work together seamlessly, give a standardised way to interact and ensure compatibility. The GUI of an operating system (windows, icons, menus, buttons) is a user interface.

Same screen, two designs (slide 6, picture only): a morning-yoga-workout app screen shown twice. The left version has ornate fonts, tiny boxed icons and long flowery copy; the right one has a clean title, plain icons, short copy and one clear "Start workout" button.

Good UX is important, but we see bad UX every day (slides 8 and 34): Arngren (www.arngren.net) is an overcrowded e-commerce interface; LINGsCARS (customer.lingscars.com) has distracting animations and visual overload; Gates N Fences (www.gatesnfences.com) has poor information organisation.

Human-computer interaction (HCI)

HCI (slide 11) involves two entities, the human and the computer, that determine each other's behaviour over time, framed by the human's goals and related tasks. It is multi-disciplinary and concerns the design, evaluation and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use. The three parts are the user, the computer or device, and the task; the system must support the user's task, with a focus on usability.

Human (slide 12)

  • The end-user of the software, other people in the organisation, the surrounding cultural context
  • Sensory systems: visual, auditory, haptic, spatial
  • Acting systems: hands, voice, head, body
  • Cognitive processes: perception, memory

Computer (slide 12)

  • The hardware and the software
  • Input peripherals: keyboard, mouse, trackpad, trackball, touch surfaces or screens, microphone, sensors
  • Output peripherals: screen, audio (voice, sounds), haptics, VR/AR headsets

Interactive system (slide 13): the combination of hardware and/or software and/or services and/or people that users interact with to achieve a specific goal. Interaction: the user tells the computer what they want and the computer communicates results. Users, with their goals, are the ultimate metric of interaction.

Moore's law and the human-computer gap

Moore's law (slide 17): transistor counts double every two years, so computational power grows exponentially. It also applies to RAM size and pixel densities. The slide's conclusion: interfaces become more and more important. The chart on the slide ("48 Years of Microprocessor Trend Data") plots transistors, single-thread performance, frequency, typical power and number of logical cores from 1970 to 2020.

History of HCI: three stages

User experience

User experience (slide 20): how people interact with a product or service; all aspects of the end-user's interaction with the company, its services and its products; the overall experience a customer or user has with a product, system or service. Don Norman, who coined the term (slide 22): no product is an island; a product is more than the product, a cohesive, integrated set of experiences across every stage, from initial intentions to final reflections, from first use to help, service and maintenance, all working together seamlessly.

UX design (slide 23): making products that people find easy and fun to use, while considering their feelings. UX designers cover the who, why, what and how of product use. UX designs must consider the context of use (slide 30): training required, conventions, laws and regulation, competing products, human abilities and limitations.

The 4Cs of UX design

C3 is "Context" but talks about contentThe slide explains Context through content (text, images, videos, clickable elements) and giving users accurate information, evoking emotion and helping them reach goals. Do not confuse it with the "context of use" on slide 30.

The formal definition: ISO 9241-210

Not just "was it usable"The ISO wording covers anticipated use and the time before and after use, and it includes emotions, beliefs and comfort. A definition that stops at "easy to use" is incomplete.

UX vs UI, and the myths about good design

User interface (slide 31): how people interact with products, covering all visual and informational elements and the navigational and organisational features. Per Nielsen, the UI is the collection of visual and interactive elements in a digital product, such as buttons, icons and layouts, while UX covers the user's entire interaction with the product. In the broad sense, UI is all components of an interactive system (software or hardware) that provide information and controls for the user to accomplish specific tasks.

Four myths about good design (slides 33 and 35)

  • Myth 1: good design is just cool graphics. Graphics are part of a bigger picture of what to communicate and how; also consider what people are trying to do and how to improve it.
  • Myth 2: good design is just common sense. Why are there so many bad websites and hard-to-use apps? The myth underestimates the work needed for great design.
  • Myth 3: the marketing department takes care of understanding customer needs. It does not help you understand behaviour: what people say versus what they do, and what they actually need.
  • Myth 4: we can fix the interface at the end. It overlooks the cost of major changes; good design includes making sure you have the right feature.

Human-AI collaboration (HAIC)

Human-AI collaboration (HAIC) (slides 37 and 39): humans and AI working together to achieve optimal outcomes, a cooperation model in which both leverage their respective strengths to jointly complete tasks and solve problems. Combining human creativity, empathy and contextual judgment with AI's speed, precision and data-processing power lets teams solve complex problems more effectively.

Example (slide 39): AI analyses large amounts of medical data and gives diagnostic suggestions to doctors, who add clinical experience and patient background. Effective human-machine collaboration uses AI's computing power and human expertise to improve the accuracy and efficiency of decision-making.

Human-centered AI (HCAI)

HCAI (slide 41, citing IBM 2026): an emerging discipline intent on creating AI systems that amplify and augment rather than displace human abilities. It seeks to preserve human control so that AI meets our needs while operating transparently, delivering equitable outcomes and respecting privacy. The slide's spiral picture runs through Understanding, Design and Development, and Evaluation, with user involvement throughout, and groups Purpose (automation, AI-autonomy, augmentation), AI properties (oversight, comprehension, integrity) and Values (ethical, protection, performance).

HCAI stakeholders (slide 43, picture only) fall into five groups. Users differ by ages and genders, abilities and expertise, cultures and ethnicities, languages and personalities. Researchers include industrial researchers, professors, educators, post-docs and graduate research assistants. Developers include software engineers, product managers, designers, programmers and evaluators. Business leaders include managers, marketers and chief technology officers. Policy-makers include police, prosecutors, judges, government staffers, regulators and research funders. Journalists and lawyers also appear; the picture groups people by position, so the exact assignment is not stated.

Control and automation are not a trade-offThe grid's two axes are independent. The goal is the top-right corner, not a point on the line between human control and automation, and the grey bands mark excess of either one ("Excessive human control", "Excessive automation").

Attributes of HCAI systems (slide 46, word cloud). The three largest: reliable, safe, trustworthy. Smaller: predictable, private, secure, unbiased, fair, controllable, directable, usable, observable, comprehensible, explainable, transparent, interpretable, dependable, robust, agile, resilient, available, traceable, trackable, auditable.

Why HCAI is essential (slide 44 infographic): it bridges the gap between technology's potential and human needs. Five points: continuous learning from human interaction; human-machine collaboration; adaptability and personalization; intuitive interfaces and accessibility; ethics and trust.

AI architecture

Architecture for scalable and user-centered AI systems
The idea

An AI system is several cooperating components, not a model bolted to an interface, and the way they are organised decides the experience: how fast answers arrive, how reliable the service is, how much users can understand and control. User expectations such as fast, reliable and trustworthy must be turned into measurable architectural requirements. The lecture walks through the five layers, how to scale them, how to organise and deploy them, and how to evaluate the result with both technical and user-centred metrics.

Why architecture determines the experience of AI

A typical production AI system includes user-facing applications, backend services, AI inference capabilities, data repositories and computing infrastructure (slide 4). AI system architecture describes how data sources, models, application services, interfaces and infrastructure are organised to deliver intelligent functionality, and how they communicate, exchange information, process requests and respond to users (slide 5).

Architecture determines (slide 6): how fast your product learns from users, how easily your models integrate with your data, and how quickly your team turns feedback into product improvements. An accurate model can still give an unsatisfactory experience if predictions arrive too late to support meaningful decisions (slide 5).

Benefits of an architecture design pattern (slide 6): reusability (apply proven solutions repeatedly), scalability (support increasing users and workloads), reliability (fewer failures through well-tested designs), maintainability (simpler future development and upgrades), and interview readiness (structured architectural thinking).

AI systems as socio-technical architectures

AI solutions operate within workflows and can shape people, policies, organisational responsibilities and environmental conditions (slide 8). They go beyond model accuracy: human interpretation, data quality, decision authority, organisational procedures and operational constraints all decide whether the application does good. An AI solution should accommodate different levels of human expertise, attention, risk tolerance and confidence, and support human oversight, user feedback and responsible decision-making. Example: a hospital AI system must fit clinical workflows, not just generate predictions.

From UX principles to architectural requirements

The UX principles are usability, accessibility, responsiveness, transparency and trust (slide 10). In architectural design they become requirements that shape software components, communication mechanisms and infrastructure. Responsive interaction requires efficient inference services and suitable resource allocation. User transparency requires evidence retrieval, explanation services or model-output metadata. Accessible interaction requires support for multiple input and output modalities. NB: architectural decisions should translate abstract UX expectations into implementable and measurable system capabilities.

Architectural design (slide 13) shapes how the system behaves in real operation: (1) turn usability into engineering requirements, such as a specified percentile of requests completing within an agreed time limit; (2) transparency: meaningful explanations, which may need evidence retrieval, feature attribution, model metadata and explanation presentation; (3) accessibility can shape component interfaces and the interaction workflow; (4) trust and reliability: users expect the system to be dependable, predictable and able to communicate its limitations; (5) interface and system evaluation: evaluating the interface alone cannot show that the whole application meets user needs, so combine UX testing with infrastructure monitoring, model evaluation, safety analysis and real-task performance measurements.

User-centered design, stakeholders and requirements

Human-centered design puts users' needs, abilities and expectations at the centre (slide 14). AI architecture should begin with understanding user goals, not with choosing technologies; users should take part in requirement gathering, prototype evaluation and usability testing; the design is continuously improved from feedback and observed behaviour. Example: testing an AI healthcare dashboard with clinicians can reveal missing features before full deployment.

Different users, different needs (slide 15), for a university AI assistant: students need useful answers and clear navigation; administrators need tools to update institutional information; technical personnel need deployment, monitoring and maintenance capabilities; security personnel may need audit information and access-control mechanisms.

Requirements that influence architectural decisions (slide 16)

  • Functional: system capabilities, such as generating predictions, explanations or recommendations; what the system must do, such as answering questions or retrieving documents.
  • Nonfunctional: performance expectations such as scalability, security, availability and response time; how effectively it must perform.
  • Operational: constraints on infrastructure, maintenance, security and institutional responsibilities.
  • Responsible-AI: privacy, transparency, fairness, accountability and appropriate human oversight.

User-centered capabilities need architectural support (slides 18 and 19)

  • Explainability: evidence retrieval, feature attribution or explanation-generation components.
  • Accessibility: support for text, speech, screen readers and alternative interaction formats, for different physical, cognitive, linguistic and technological needs.
  • Human control: review, correction, approval or escalation services.
  • Personalisation: adapts behaviour using relevant user preferences and context.
  • Feedback mechanisms: users correct outputs, report problems, suggest improvements.
Three or four?Slide 15 says the needs "can be grouped into three categories", but slide 16 lists four bullets (functional, nonfunctional, operational, responsible-AI). Learn all four as the slides list them. Slide 19's example: a learning assistant can offer simplified explanations, speech output and personalised exercises while protecting learner information.

Architectural decisions and UX trade-offs

Architectural patterns should be selected according to actual requirements rather than technological popularity (slide 20). A small AI application may work well as a modular monolith; a large platform may benefit from independently scalable services. Cloud inference gives centralised computing resources, edge inference can reduce network dependence; each alternative has trade-offs.

Do not maximise every metricIncreasing computational capacity may reduce response times but raises operating costs and energy consumption (slide 21). Architects evaluate each decision against actual user needs rather than automatically maximising every technical performance metric.

Architectural qualities

Scalable versus user-centred architecture (slide 23): scalable means able to take increasing workloads, data volumes and computational requirements; user-centred means components organised around the needs, expectations, abilities and operating contexts of users. Both are essential. Example: a university assistant may give good answers to 100 students and become extremely slow when several thousand ask at once.

  • Elasticity (slide 24): adjust allocated computing resources to changing demand. A cloud chatbot raises inference capacity during exams and lowers it when demand falls.
  • Reliability (slide 24): perform intended functions consistently under specified conditions; vital where users depend on accurate, timely, repeatable behaviour.
  • Availability (slide 25): operational and accessible when needed. Adaptability: accommodate new requirements, datasets, models, interfaces and contexts; modular interfaces and well-defined data contracts make it easier to replace a model without redesigning the whole application.

The five layers of a user-centered AI system

Application layer (slide 29): coordinates communication between the interface, data services, AI models and external systems. An API is a defined mechanism for sending requests and receiving responses. Orchestration coordinates multiple processing steps and sets the order in which services run; an orchestration engine may coordinate components for document retrieval, calculations, database queries and content generation. Good orchestration makes complex AI functionality appear coherent and manageable to users.

AI processing and inference

  1. Training creates model parameters from suitable datasets.
  2. Evaluation decides whether the model meets defined performance requirements.
  3. A model registry stores approved model versions and their metadata.
  4. Deployment makes a selected model available for inference.

Treat training and inference as distinct workloads: they have different processing and operational characteristics (slide 30). Model versioning and controlled updates reduce the risk of disrupting production services. Inference architecture (slide 31) decides how trained models process incoming requests. A basic service receives input, runs the model and returns a result; at larger scale it may need multiple servers, specialised GPUs, load balancing and intelligent request scheduling. Distributed inference uses multiple computing instances to serve AI requests.

The data layer, generative AI and RAG

Data architecture (slide 32) decides how information enters an AI system and becomes available. Typical components: databases, object storage, document repositories, processing pipelines and search indexes. Generative applications may use embedding models to turn text into numerical representations called vectors. A university chatbot may search institutional documents for registration rules; retrieval quality depends on document preparation, indexing strategy, search methods and access controls. The data layer must also manage document updates, data quality and information provenance, and distinguish current, outdated, public and restricted information.

Reliable and highly available systems

Reliability is whether the application performs its intended functions consistently; availability is whether it stays operational and accessible (slide 36). AI systems depend on many services, so partial failures matter: a language model may be up while retrieval fails, or retrieval may work while the application API is overloaded. Reliable architecture needs monitoring and recovery across components. Common techniques: redundancy, health checks, controlled retries, failover, service isolation. NB: reliability evaluation should cover both operational availability and functional service quality.

Reliability engineering principles (slide 37)

  • Fault prevention: design systems to minimise failures.
  • Fault tolerance: keep functioning despite component failures.
  • Fault isolation: contain failures to prevent system-wide impact.
  • Fault prediction: anticipate failures through analysis and monitoring.

Common techniques (slide 36)

Technologies and frameworks for web front-end development

Front-end tech · what runs in the browser, and what it does to the user's experience
The idea

The front end is what the user sees and touches. It is built from HTML (content), CSS (presentation) and JavaScript (behaviour), and frameworks, rendering strategies and performance measures sit on top. New technology appears when user needs outgrow the old one, but a newer technology does not automatically give a better experience: it must be judged against complexity and the real requirements.

How a web application works

Web development is creating and maintaining websites and web applications: designing user interfaces, processing data and connecting components. The steps of one visit: the user opens the site in a browser, the browser sends a request to the web server, the server processes it and may use a database or other services, the server sends a response, and the browser processes it and shows the page.

  • Client-server: a web application has a client (usually a browser) and one or more servers. HTTP defines how requests and responses are exchanged.
  • The browser requests resources, interprets HTML, applies CSS, runs JavaScript and shows interactive content.
  • The server (a backend service) processes requests, applies business rules, reaches protected information and returns responses.
  • Developer roles: front-end, back-end and full-stack developers (slide 9).

Accessibility, semantic HTML and forms

Accessibility means a site is usable by people of all abilities. Following the web accessibility standards (the W3C's WCAG) is described as a legal requirement that also improves SEO, brand value and market share. The four principles are known as POUR:

Semantic HTML gives content its meaning, so browsers and assistive technologies can interpret content and interaction. Example from the slide: a native button has built-in keyboard and focus behaviour that developers would otherwise have to rebuild.

Forms: prevent errors early

  • Forms collect structured input: text inputs, checkboxes, radio buttons, selection lists, submit buttons.
  • Client-side validation (JavaScript, HTML validation features) checks that required data is present before the request is sent.
  • Validation done only in the browser is insufficient for security, because requests can be modified or built independently of the page. The slide words this as "server-side validation performed in the browser"; the point is that the server must check too.

Good error handling

  • An error message identifies the problem and explains how to correct it.
  • When validation fails, keep the correctly entered values instead of clearing the form.
  • A validated form does not prove that AI-generated recommendations are correct.

HTML, CSS, JavaScript and the DOM

  • HTML represents content and semantic organisation: headings, paragraphs, forms, buttons, links.
  • CSS controls how elements appear: colour, typography, spacing, responsive layout, alignment, and the visual relationships between interface elements.
  • JavaScript lets the application react to user actions and update the page dynamically: it validates inputs, manipulates the document, talks to APIs and displays recommendations.
  • The browser parses HTML and CSS to build the structures used for rendering, while JavaScript can modify content and behaviour.
  • DOM (Document Object Model) is a programming interface for web documents. It represents the page as nodes and objects so programs can change the structure, style and content.
  • Events: a click, tap, drag, text entry or form submission creates an event. JavaScript registers event listeners that run functions when the event occurs. Why CSS matters, and where it fails

  • HTML describes meaning and structure; CSS describes appearance. This lets developers change presentation without rewriting the document, and one rule can style many elements consistently.

From static pages to dynamic applications

A static website serves prepared resources (HTML, CSS, JavaScript, images). The server does not necessarily generate a new personalised HTML document for each request.

Advantages

  • Fast loading, predictable navigation, minimal dependence on JavaScript.
  • Effective for users who want stable information rather than complex interaction.

Disadvantages

  • More complex interaction needs browser-side code, external APIs or server processing.
  • No inherent personalised server-side processing.

JavaScript (standardised as ECMAScript) is a high-level language that makes sites dynamic and interactive: it manipulates information, computes, responds to events and talks to external services. It can enable some interactions without a server request and update parts of a page as information changes. Trade-off: more complexity, loading cost and opportunities for error.

Direct DOM manipulation works for small interfaces, but coordinating many linked updates gets hard. That limit pushed developers to higher-level component and state-management abstractions.

Security, privacy and streaming AI output

Security and privacy (slide 47)

  • Users can inspect and modify downloaded JavaScript, form values and network requests.
  • Secret API keys and privileged credentials must not be embedded in client-side JavaScript.
  • Rendering untrusted information as executable HTML can introduce Cross-Site Scripting (XSS). AI-generated text and API response strings are untrusted data. Use text-rendering for ordinary text and carefully controlled sanitisation where rich content is truly needed.
  • Data minimisation: collect only the information the defined user task needs.
  • Security and privacy affect user confidence, but the safeguards must also be understandable.

Streaming AI outputs (slide 48)

  • Traditional interfaces show information only after the full server response; long generative operations leave users waiting with nothing useful on screen.
  • A browser can consume response data progressively and update the display. UX gain: earlier visible feedback, lengthy processing feels more responsive.
  • Streamed text must not be confused with a finalised or verified result. Distinguish content still being generated from output that has completed processing and any required checks.
  • Streams can stop (connectivity loss, service failure, cancellation). Indicate incomplete output and give retry or continuation options.

User-centred design principles

User-centred design (UCD) · research the users first, then design, test and repeat
The idea

User-centred design lets the users' characteristics, needs, goals, tasks and contexts guide every design decision, so that systems help people reach meaningful goals effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily, not just deliver technically correct functions. It runs as a cycle: understand the context of use, specify requirements, produce designs, evaluate them with users, and iterate.

What UCD is, and why AI needs it

  • Definition: UCD is an approach in which the characteristics, needs, goals, tasks and contexts of the intended users guide design decisions throughout system development.
  • Goal: systems that help people achieve meaningful goals effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily, rather than only delivering technically correct functionality.
  • For AI: predictions and recommendations may be sophisticated, but they must be presented through interfaces that users can understand, evaluate and control.
  • Evidence over assumptions: decisions should come from observations, interviews, usability evaluations and other evidence, not only from developers' assumptions.

Fundamental philosophy (slide 8): put the needs, goals, characteristics and context of intended users at the centre.

  • Investigate real user problems before deciding whether a technology or feature is a suitable solution.
  • Representative users contribute throughout: research, observation, prototype evaluation, usability testing.
  • Account for human capabilities and limits: perception, memory, attention, physical interaction, accessibility requirements.
  • Repeat evaluation and refinement, and reconsider requirements and solutions when new evidence appears.
Warm-up case: the team that trusted itselfA university AI assistant recommends courses; the team believed the interface was intuitive, yet students cannot understand the recommendations or check prerequisites. The lesson: before designing, study the intended users, tasks and context with real research instead of assuming the interface is intuitive.

A design goal says what the proposed system should help its intended users accomplish. Usability is how effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily specified users achieve specified goals in a particular context of use.

The human-centred design process: a continuous cycle

  1. Understand the context of use: investigate intended users, their tasks, environments, current practices and relevant constraints.
  2. Specify user requirements: turn research findings into explicit statements of the capabilities and qualities the system must provide.
  3. Produce design solutions: sketches, interaction flows, wireframes and prototypes that address the requirements.
  4. Evaluate the proposed solutions: watch users do realistic tasks to find difficulties and see whether the design meets the requirements.
  5. Iterate when necessary: use findings to refine the interface, revise earlier assumptions, or research requirements that are still unclear.

An alternative picture (slide 21): "what is wanted" (interviews, ethnography; what is there versus what is wanted) leads to analysis (scenarios, task analysis), then design (guidelines, principles), a prototype (dialog notations; evaluation and heuristics) that loops back to analysis, and finally implement and deploy (architectures, documentation, help) after a precise specification. Evaluation also decides whether to move on to implementation or repeat earlier steps (slide 46).

User research: who the users are and how to study them

User research is the systematic investigation of users' goals, behaviours, experiences, expectations, needs and interactions with existing or proposed systems. Its findings guide interface organisation, functionality, accessibility, explanations, feedback and user control. For AI it should also study how users interpret recommendations, respond to uncertainty, and decide whether to rely on outputs.

Needfinding is finding potential user needs by asking: Who are the users? How are they doing it now? In what context? Who are the users of the system: uniform, or different categories? Young or old, novice or experienced? Do not think of "generic" users: split the categories.

  2. Read all your data from beginning to end.
  3. Code the text based on what it is about.
  4. Create new codes to encapsulate potential themes.

Triangulation examines a research question through complementary methods, sources or perspectives to strengthen understanding. Interviews show how users interpret, observation shows actual behaviour, task measurements quantify performance. Findings that disagree across methods must be investigated, because the gap may expose misunderstandings.

Personas, empathy maps and journey maps

Personas show how different users approach the same task with different experience, knowledge and support needs. A useful persona stresses the characteristics that influence interaction decisions, and helps requirements, scenarios, interface concepts and evaluation tasks by keeping user needs visible. To build one: analyse research data for recurring patterns in goals, behaviours, task experience and interaction requirements; give each persona a clear primary goal; add context (prior experience, technology familiarity, language needs, devices, institutional constraints) and the difficulties and motivations that explain how they approach tasks.

A persona holds: Goals (what the user is trying to achieve), Behaviour (patterns that reveal goals), Attitudes (that predict behaviour), Motivations (why), and Business objectives (what you ideally want users to do so the business value succeeds).

  • Empathy map: a collaborative research artefact that organises information about a user or user group into what they say (what the user explicitly states), think (questions, concerns and beliefs supported by research), do (observable actions) and feel (emotions supported by evidence). It also covers their expectations of the product and the difficulties they face. The NN/g template (slide 33) adds a goal area: who we are empathising with, what they need to do; plus see, hear, pains and gains.
  • Chelsea's key insights (slide 34): values flexibility and convenience because of work and family; seeks practical, career-relevant skills with clear benefits; needs trustworthy guidance; may be uncertain about time, workload and relevance. Design opportunities: recommend flexible courses that fit her evenings and weekends; personalised, career-aligned recommendations; clear workload estimates and learning outcomes; explanations of why a course is recommended; easy comparison and the ability to save and review options.

User journey mapping visually represents the sequence of experiences a user goes through to reach a specific goal. It identifies the main stages, actions, decisions and touchpoints, captures thoughts, emotions, expectations and difficulties, and shows how experience changes over time. It helps designers spot confusing interactions, unnecessary steps, unmet needs and critical moments that can block task completion.

Stage 2: specify user requirements

Transform research findings into explicit statements of the capabilities and qualities the system must provide. Researchers outline usage scenarios through a sitemap, whose purpose is to map out the menu and features within an application. Alternative ways to frame requirements (slide 40): HMW (How Might We...), Opportunity Solution Tree, Jobs To Be Done (JTBD), and personas.

  • User scenario: a realistic situation in which a particular user tries to reach a specific goal with the system, considering needs and context.
  • Task flow: visually represents the sequence of actions and decisions a user follows to complete a task. It should include important decisions, unsuccessful outcomes, and chances to correct information or choose another approach.
  • The "Design in reality" picture (slide 42): your plan is a smooth road to the finish; reality is full of obstacles. It supports planning for failure paths and iteration.
  • Ideation (slide 43, "Design Thinking: prototyping"): decide which ideation techniques to use: brainstorming, mind mapping, rapid prototyping. The slide picture is an impact-effort matrix with the cells Do, Decide, Delegate, Delete.

Stage 3: design solutions and iterative design

  • Design ideas are generated and represented as sketches, wireframes or initial prototypes. The process can be iterative, with many revisions based on user feedback. The result of the UI design process is a prototype that addresses the identified requirements.
  • Iterative design repeatedly develops, evaluates and improves a proposed solution based on evidence from users and other constraints. Each iteration begins with a clear objective or hypothesis about a problem the design intends to address.
  • Prototypes let teams evaluate individual ideas before investing in full implementation and deployment.

Stage 4: evaluate with usability testing

Evaluation checks whether the interface design meets user needs and expectations, and decides whether to go on to system implementation or repeat earlier steps. Usability testing evaluates how representative users interact with an interface while attempting realistic tasks. Its purpose is to find problems in the design, not to judge participants' intelligence or technical competence. For AI applications it should examine task completion, understanding of recommendations, appropriate reliance, user control and error recovery.

Planning a usability test (slide 48)

  • State what you want to evaluate, e.g. whether users understand AI recommendations, complete tasks and recover from errors.
  • Pick users who reflect the intended audience: goals, experience, technical ability, accessibility needs.
  • Write scenarios based on real user goals, without telling participants which buttons to click.
  • Prepare the prototype, environment, instructions, consent process, observation methods and consistent task-completion criteria.
  • Decide how effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, errors and task recovery will be evaluated.

Conducting it (slide 49)

  • Explain the purpose, get informed consent, and reassure participants that the interface is evaluated, not their abilities.
  • Present tasks as real goals, without revealing controls or steps.
  • Record actions, hesitation, errors, repeated attempts, requests for help and outcomes, without needless interruption.
  • No leading questions, no hints; use neutral prompts to learn expectations and difficulties.
  • Document observations systematically; ask neutral follow-ups to learn the reasons behind actions.

Prioritising problems and planning the next iteration (slides 51-52)

  1. Identify usability problems: confusing navigation, unclear AI recommendations, user errors, unsuccessful tasks.
  2. Assess severity by frequency, impact on task completion, consequences for users and difficulty of recovery.
  3. Prioritise critical issues: fix problems that cause consequential errors, block task completion or significantly affect users before minor visual inconsistencies.
  4. Plan design improvements: turn findings into specific changes such as clearer explanations, better navigation, error prevention, more effective feedback.
  5. Evaluate the next iteration: test the revised interface with representative users to see whether the changes fix the problems without creating new ones.
Efficiency is not satisfactionEfficiency is objective resources (time, effort, number of actions). Satisfaction is the subjective feeling (ease of use, confidence, comfort). Slide 50 adds user errors as a fourth thing to measure besides the three usability goals.

Design thinking

Applying design thinking to AI projects · understand people before you choose the technology
The idea

Design thinking is a human-centred way to solve complex, poorly defined problems. It runs through Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test and Iterate, and it asks first whether AI is needed at all. Model accuracy is not the same as a good user experience, so every AI concept is tested with real tasks and revised from evidence.

From user-centred design to design thinking

Characteristics of design thinking

  • Empathy-driven: understand user needs and experiences.
  • Iterative: several rounds of ideation, prototyping and testing.
  • Collaborative: cross-functional teamwork.
  • Solution-oriented: find innovative solutions to complex problems.

Empathy is the foundational principle: a disciplined way to investigate and address uncertainty.

The stages of design thinking

  • Empathize: research users' needs: goals, motivations, behaviours, problems and contexts.
  • Define: formulate the right problem; state clearly what is to be solved.
  • Ideate: generate a wide range of ideas, solutions or assumptions, and investigate whether AI provides meaningful value.
  • Prototype: sketches, interface mockups, simulated AI recommendations, interaction demonstrations or other artefacts that can be evaluated.
  • Test: try the solutions out by evaluating ideas with users.
  • Iterate: revisit earlier activities when necessary.

The Double Diamond

Budgets are limited and deadlines tight, so a process must find and solve problems strategically without wasting time or money. The Double Diamond gives a framework for clearly identifying a problem and delivering a relevant solution. The Design Council developed it in 2005, built on divergent and convergent thinking.

Empathize: understand people before designing AI

  • Investigate what users are trying to accomplish, why the task matters and which circumstances shape their behaviour.
  • Separate user goals from requested features. A student asking for an AI chatbot may really need understandable academic guidance, reliable information, or a faster way to compare courses.
  • Context strongly influences needs and decisions; understanding the existing experience reveals opportunities.
  • Include diverse users and stakeholders: technical experience, accessibility needs, responsibilities, cultural backgrounds, expectations of AI.

Unmet needs. Not every observation is a universal requirement. One working professional's problem is a lead, not a rule. Useful insights lead to actionable opportunities, illustrated with data. Multimodal UX

Multimodal UX design · voice, touch and gesture, chosen and coordinated
The idea

Multimodal UX is not about adding more ways to interact. It is about selecting, combining and coordinating voice, touch, gesture and feedback so users can work with an AI system naturally, reliably and accessibly. Each modality has its own strengths and failure modes, so the design must handle context, conflicts, fallbacks, devices and accessibility.

After this lecture you should be able to (slide 3): explain the principles of voice, touch and gesture interaction; design intuitive, accessible interfaces with suitable modalities; describe how multimodal systems combine inputs, keep context and coordinate feedback; apply modality-switching and error-recovery strategies across devices; and evaluate multimodal interfaces by recognition accuracy, task efficiency, accessibility and interaction reliability.

What multimodal design is

  • Definition: crafting UI experiences with several modes of interaction such as voice, text or haptic feedback. The main goal is to improve the interaction and the user's engagement.
  • Not "more is better". Users may speak commands, use touchscreen controls, move their hands, listen to spoken output, or receive visual and tactile feedback.
  • Different modalities suit different things: voice for natural-language requests, touch for direct control of visible elements, contactless gestures where touching a device is inconvenient.
  • Noise, lighting, device size, motor abilities, privacy expectations and available attention decide which modalities are practical.
  • Modalities can work independently, sequentially or simultaneously, depending on task and design.
  • It is not the same as using several devices: a smartphone already offers touchscreen, microphone, speakers and haptic motor.
  • Success needs meaningful coordination: preserve context, interpret commands appropriately, give consistent feedback, and let users continue when one modality becomes unavailable.
  • Why it matters in AI applications: more ways to express intent; different modalities for different conditions (voice when hands are busy, touch in noisy places or when privacy matters); support for diverse abilities, provided the same task is not made inaccessible in the alternative; better contextual communication; less dependence on complicated navigation.
Warm-up answerOffering voice, touch and gesture does not automatically make the AI Course Planner easier to use. More modalities do not automatically give better UX: the benefit comes from the appropriate interaction being available for the task and context.

Voice-based UX

A voice user interface (VUI) lets users interact through spoken language: commands, questions, requests, answers, without relying only on a graphical interface. It supports hands-free and eyes-free activity and may be command-based or conversational. It is weak for presenting large amounts of detail and adds uncertainty in speech recognition and language interpretation. Examples: smart speakers, voice-enabled navigation, telephone assistants, accessibility tools, conversational AI assistants.

Voice assistant system (slide 14) shows these blocks: voice input, natural language understanding, integration with third-party services, response generation, user authentication, task feedback and user feedback.

  • Voice commands: use the words users naturally associate with their goals; support linguistic variation; say what requests the system can handle; keep responses concise and structured; avoid needless steps; distinguish commands with significant consequences from ordinary information requests.
  • Example. Less effective: "The system has identified twelve course recommendations corresponding to your current query parameters." Improved: "I found three online business courses that may fit your evening schedule. Would you like to hear about them or see them on screen?"
  • Conversation: turn-taking decides when user and system speak. Give clear cues (visual indicator, brief sound, short acknowledgement) that speech was detected. Dialogue management supports follow-up questions. Barge-in lets users interrupt system speech; it saves waiting but the system must tell intentional speech from background talk and its own audio. Support clarification without excessive repetition.
  • Feedback: short acknowledgements, spoken descriptions or visual state changes; confirmations proportional to the action's importance; audio cues for simple states; long results disclosed progressively; no doubt about whether the action completed; coordinate with visual and tactile feedback.

Speech errors and ambiguity

  • Recognition errors: the transcript differs from what was said (a word replaced, omitted or added). Interpretation errors can occur even when the transcript is right.
  • Distinguish uncertainty from failure: ask for clarification when ambiguous. Repeated failures must change strategy. Important actions need extra protection against misinterpretation.
  • Evaluate with both transcription and task measures. Low word error rate is useful but not enough.
Word Error Rate (standard ASR metric)WER = (S + D + I) / N × 100%S = substitutions · D = deletions · I = insertions · N = words in the reference transcript

Challenges: background noise; different performance across languages, accents and speaking styles; voice-only interaction can be inaccessible or inconvenient; microphone access creates privacy issues. When the microphone is unreliable, offer touch, text or another alternative while preserving the user's progress.

Designing gesture commands

  • Understandable link between gesture and action (hand right = advance), clearly defined and consistent.
  • Keep the vocabulary manageable: many similar poses cause confusion.
  • Comfortable and practical to repeat.
  • Show when a gesture was recognised.
  • Separate recognition from execution when errors have consequences.
  • Keep alternatives: touch, physical buttons, accessible navigation.

Challenges

  • Sensor limitations.
  • Lighting and background conditions.
  • Physical fatigue.
  • Unintentional gestures trigger false actions.
  • Performance varies across users.
  • Privacy and accessibility concerns.

Choosing the right modality

Start from the task. A natural-language search suits speech; picking one of several visible options is more efficient by touch. Also weigh the amount and complexity of information, the precision required (precise text entry, careful value adjustment and small targets are hard by contactless gesture without extra controls), device capabilities, and environment: speech is less practical in noisy or private settings, camera gestures suffer from lighting, occlusion and space. Interaction patterns and input fusion

When

  • Sequential: different modalities at different stages of a task.
  • Simultaneous: modalities give related information at about the same time.

How the inputs relate

  • Complementary: each gives a different piece of one intended action.
  • Redundant: similar information through more than one modality.
  • Independent: does not necessarily need fusion.

The pattern decides how input events are combined. Multimodal fusion combines information from different sources (speech, touch, gesture) to determine intent.

The multimodal AI pipeline on slide 37 has eight steps: ingest inputs (text, image, audio, video, sensors), preprocess data, encode each modality, align related signals (in time, space or meaning), fuse information (early fusion, late fusion, attention or cross-modal, hybrid), reason or classify, generate output (text, visual, audio, decision or action), and evaluate the result (accuracy, confidence, relevance, safety and bias check, human review when needed, feedback loop). The slide's second graphic lists six steps: data collection; feature removal; combining different modalities; training models; generation and inference; recommendations and enhancements.

Context, conflicts, switching and fallback

  • Interaction context holds what is needed to interpret the current request; system state is the application's current condition. Context lets users switch modality without repeating information.
  • Referential commands tie language to visible objects ("compare these two"). Context changes must be synchronised across components, and context should not persist inappropriately.
  • Conflicting inputs example: the student says "Select Course A" while tapping Course B. The system may prioritise deliberate direct selection, but must not automatically submit an enrolment when voice and touch name different courses. Output must not give contradictory feedback, and feedback should state both the recognised action and its result. If unsure, show the alternatives clearly.
  • Modality switching changes how users interact while continuing the same task, and must preserve relevant context. Fallback provides an alternative when a modality becomes unsuitable or unavailable, without forcing repetition. Switching must be visible and understandable. Accessibility requires alternatives with equivalent task functionality where appropriate. WCAG 2.2 has guidance on concurrent input mechanisms and on not restricting input methods unnecessarily.

Cross-device consistency and adaptive interfaces

  • Consistent interaction does not require identical layouts. Task concepts keep their meaning, and application state is preserved.
  • Privacy on shared devices: personal preferences, account details and sensitive information should not automatically appear on a public kiosk just because they were used on a personal device.
  • Adaptive multimodal interfaces adjust interaction to available capabilities and context. Device characteristics shape feedback (a smartwatch gives short notifications; a tablet shows detailed comparisons). Users should understand and control important adaptations: the reason is clear and the change reversible where practical. Accessibility, privacy and environment

  • Accessibility covers different sensory, motor, cognitive and communication abilities: a convenient modality for one user may be unavailable to another.
  • Essential functions must not depend only on speech. Spoken instructions, confirmations and errors must also exist in a form for users who cannot hear them.
  • Contactless gesture must not be the only way to complete essential tasks: limited arm movement, tremors, fatigue or posture problems may need touch, keyboard, switch or other input. Complex touch gestures need simpler alternatives.

Evaluating multimodal UX

  • Use measures that fit each input method: speech errors, gesture failures, touch mistakes and cross-modal conflicts need different techniques.
  • Task completion across modalities and combinations: do users reach the same goal by voice, touch, gesture or a mixed workflow?
  • Recognition performance shows technical reliability: Word Error Rate for speech transcription; precision, recall and related measures for gesture detection or classification.
  • Interaction efficiency includes the time for recognition and recovery. A modality that is good in ideal conditions can become inefficient if users keep correcting it or switching input.
  • Include modality switching, conflict resolution and fallback effectiveness.
  • Takeaway: let users interact through the most appropriate modality while keeping consistency, accessibility and control throughout.

AR & VR

Augmented and virtual reality · designing for space, body and comfort
The idea

AR adds digital content to the real world, VR replaces most of it with a simulation, and MR stresses how tightly the two interact. Good immersive UX is not realistic graphics: it is meaningful tasks, reliable spatial alignment, comfort, safety and user control. AI helps by recognising objects and guiding users, but the human must stay in control.

AR, VR, MR and XR: what each one is

Augmented reality (AR) lets designers enhance perception of the real, physical world with computer-generated content. Virtual reality (VR) creates a computer-generated environment, a simulation of an alternate world, that users can explore and interact with; it uses equipment such as computers, sensors, headsets and gloves. Extended reality (XR) is the umbrella term for technologies that combine or simulate physical and digital experiences. It commonly includes AR, VR and MR, but the exact boundaries vary across platforms and the technical literature.

  • Instruction: AR-enhanced instruction and training provide real-time information and diagrams.
  • Interaction: AR can replace physical controls with virtual control panels.

New UX considerations: opportunities and challenges

Designers must account for spatial orientation, depth perception, viewpoint, virtual-object placement, user movement, physical comfort and environmental safety. AI contributes contextual assistance: computer vision identifies relevant physical objects, and intelligent virtual agents give guidance or adapt how information is shown. Visual realism alone is not the goal: systems need meaningful tasks, understandable interactions, reliable spatial alignment, comfortable use and appropriate user control.

Opportunities

  • Spatial interaction makes some information easier to grasp: a 3D machine shows locations and functions better than separate 2D diagrams.
  • Experiential learning: practise actions in realistic spatial contexts.

Challenges

  • New interaction requirements: how far objects appear, whether they can be reached comfortably, how the interface responds when users move.
  • AR depends on understanding the environment: poor lighting, too few visual features, reflective surfaces or tracking failures make objects drift, look unstable or sit in the wrong place.
  • VR must manage comfort: bad locomotion, unstable rendering, unnatural visual motion and long sessions cause discomfort.
Most impressive is not most usefulThe most visually impressive experience is not necessarily the most useful, especially for precise, demanding or consequential tasks. Preserve accessibility, task relevance and control.

Enabling technology: hardware, degrees of freedom and displays

VR hardware

  • Head-mounted display (HMD)
  • Motion tracking sensors
  • Input controllers
  • Computing unit

These are the physical foundation of any VR experience; their quality decides how convincing and comfortable it feels.

AR hardware

  • Transparent displays
  • Cameras
  • Sensors
  • Real-time rendering systems

AR may use transparent optics or camera passthrough; smartphones and tablets are accessible entry points.

Hardware shapes UX decisions. Resolution, display refresh rate, field of view, weight, tracking coverage, input precision, battery life and available physical space all affect comfort and task performance. Motion sensors estimate device orientation and movement.

Displays. A stereoscopic display shows slightly offset images to the left and right eye, mimicking binocular vision, and so creates the illusion of 3D depth (generally 1-5 minutes of arc per pixel). Field of view is the angular extent of the visible scene. Optics add limits: lens distortion, optical blur, limited resolution, and a mismatch between visual convergence and focal distance can hurt readability and comfort. Design for comfortable viewing, not exaggerated depth.

Spatial tracking, SLAM and performance

Spatial tracking combines motion sensors, cameras and processing algorithms into a continuous, real-time picture of position, orientation and movement. Sensor fusion merges several sensors into one estimate that is more accurate, more complete and more robust than any single sensor.

SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping) lets an autonomous system map a complex environment while tracking its own position. Its challenges: computational complexity, multi-sensor noise and calibration, environmental difficulty, real-time constraints, scalability, data-fusion complexity, hardware limits and robustness to drift.

Performance. Frames per second (FPS) is one of the most critical determinants of VR quality; it depends on the complexity of the virtual environment, hardware quality, rendering techniques and latency. Latency is the delay between a user's action and the system's response, and it is detrimental to the VR experience. Minimise it to keep perception stable.

UX design for AR

Environmental design vs interaction design. Environmental design is the context in which users meet your app: the device is a "window" into an augmented environment, whether they are walking down a street, at a concert or driving. Context guides UI placement, colour and size. Interaction design is how users interact inside that window (phone screen or headset viewfinder, with media and 3D objects), and context also limits which interactions are possible. Always understand the context of use.

  • Anchoring. AR elements can be placed relative to real locations or objects through spatial anchors; world-anchored content must stay visually tied to its target. Not all information should be fixed to the environment. Anchor choice must allow for tracking uncertainty and changing conditions. Example: a student points a device at a lab instrument and a label names its components, staying attached as the viewpoint changes.
  • Spatial layout. Place content at distances suited to the task. Apparent size depends on physical size and viewing distance. Spatial hierarchy should guide attention without overwhelming the scene. Text readability depends on more than font size. Headsets differ in comfortable viewing distance. Minimise unnecessary head and neck movement.
  • Clutter and attention. Three ideas on the slide: arrange the layout of overlays by spatial constraints; emphasise the scene the user attends to and show other overlays more abstractly; use focus+context.
  • Guidance for real tasks. Connect digital instructions to physical actions (identify the component, show the next step, say which part). Use manageable steps. Arrows, highlights and labels must separate the intended part from nearby alternatives. Support progress tracking and correction. Keep AI-generated guidance separate from authoritative procedural requirements. Never obstruct hazards, reduce situational awareness, or push users beyond their training or authorisation.

UX design for VR

VR replaces the immediate visual surroundings with a computer-generated world. The environment should support a clear activity, not just exploration. Keep spatial coherence, make objects behave by understandable rules, balance realism with instructional usefulness, and give clear entry, orientation, progression and exit.

  • Spatial orientation is knowing your position and direction in the virtual space: where you are, where the destinations are, how areas connect. Use landmarks, let the environment structure signal navigation options, scale navigation aids to the environment's complexity, help users recover from disorientation, and judge wayfinding by observable outcomes. Example: lab workstations with recognisable names and colours, so students find the right place for each experiment stage.
Presence vs immersionPresence is the user's state (psychological); immersion is the technology's capability (senses, resolution, richness). An examiner may swap them.

Spatial interaction, selection and manipulation

Spatial interaction lets users communicate intentions in 3D. Options: gaze-based interaction (where the user looks), hand tracking (direct manipulation), motion controllers (tracked physical input), and body movement (navigation and embodiment). Choose the technique by the spatial task.

Manipulation design. The technique should match the intended physical or simulated activity; object properties influence the design; feedback should show when an object can be manipulated and what the consequences are; very precise tasks may need interaction assistance (like the snap).

Human-AI collaboration, AI assistance and human control

Human-AI collaboration describes how people and AI together accomplish an activity. The goal is effective cooperation, not maximum automation; the right level of automation depends on the task and what happens if it goes wrong. AI assistance should help users understand the activity.

  • AI-powered immersive assistance. Computer vision identifies objects and features; spatial understanding ties guidance to the surroundings; intelligent virtual agents support interactive learning; assistance adapts to the task state. But recognition and generated guidance can be wrong, so the interface should separate verified instructions from uncertain AI suggestions.
  • Human control. Vital when virtual guidance influences physical action. Provide clear confirmation before significant actions, reliable interruption, task-appropriate communication of uncertainty, error recovery to a safe and understandable state, and thought about what incorrect automation costs.

Accessibility, comfort, safety and privacy

Accessibility. Design for a wide range of abilities. Essential tasks must not depend only on complex physical movement; immersive information should also be available through alternative channels; navigation and interaction settings should be adjustable; users must be able to recover orientation. Evaluate accessibility with users who represent the intended range of abilities. The W3C XR Accessibility User Requirements name needs around alternative input methods, orientation, motion-independent interaction, customisation and sickness avoidance.

Comfort and safety. Side effects of HMD-delivered VR include "simulator sickness", "motion sickness", "cybersickness" and "VR sickness"; cybersickness describes adverse effects from using virtual environments. Discomfort is also reported as a range of physical and psychological symptoms from wearing the headset. Fatigue and physical risks belong here too (slide title).

Privacy and ethics. AR/VR may process information about the user's physical environment. Devices can collect detailed movement and interaction data: head poses, controller movements, hand positions, activity logs. Eye tracking adds further privacy concerns. Spatial data may allow inferences about personal characteristics. The W3C WebXR specification discusses privacy from spatial reference spaces, including inferences from movement and user-height information. Consider both individual and environmental consequences.

  • Presence: the user's subjective experience of the virtual environment.

Personalisation & trends

Balancing personalisation with user preferences · and how UX research is changing
The idea

An adaptive AI system tailors the experience to each person, so the designer now governs the behaviour of a changing experience instead of drawing fixed screens. Personalisation must be balanced with user-defined constraints, control, privacy and variety, and it must be evaluated with scorecards, experiments and new research methods. Success is measured by what people can meaningfully and responsibly achieve, not by one impressive metric.

From static interfaces to adaptive AI experiences

Static interfaces give predefined content and behaviour. Adaptive AI interfaces change their outputs based on user information and context, so the link between a user's action and the system's response becomes less obvious. Adaptive systems need ongoing attention to user expectations, and UX researchers must study both human behaviour and AI system behaviour. The design objective shifts from designing every screen to governing the behaviour of a changing experience.

Personalisation vs customisation: who is in control?

Personalisation tailors experience, behaviour, content and recommendations to an individual without manual input: the system adapts to the user, proactive and data-driven. Examples: a consulting site showing industry content from browsing behaviour; a shop suggesting products from purchase history; a streaming platform curating shows from viewing habits. Customisation puts control in the user's hands: they manually adjust settings or layout. Examples: rearranging dashboard widgets, dark/light mode, filtering news or product categories. It builds satisfaction and a sense of ownership.

Preference controls. Users can inspect important stored preferences; edits give a clear, predictable response; a reset recovers from unwanted personalisation; controls separate temporary adjustments from persistent changes; management stays understandable.

Cold start is the problem a system faces with no prior data about a new user, product or content item, so it cannot give meaningful personalised results from learned behaviour. Three variants:

  • User cold start: a new user has no interaction history. Initial preference questions help.
  • Item cold start: a new product, content item or creative has not been seen by enough users to have reliable engagement data.
  • System cold start: a new personalisation platform is deployed with no historical data at all.

Short vs long term. Short-term intent is what the user is trying to do in this interaction; long-term preferences are relatively persistent goals or interests. They can conflict. Session-aware personalisation stresses recent context; long-term models give continuity across sessions. Combining them needs an explicit weighting or decision strategy, and a system should not automatically give history more authority than a current explicit request. A system should also not assume old preferences stay valid forever; adaptation balances responsiveness with stability.

Dynamic profiles, preference drift and context

Dynamic user profile. Unlike a static profile filled in at registration, it can take in updated goals, current context and new evidence. Categorise attributes by origin and reliability; use timestamps (a two-year-old preference may deserve reconsideration); separate confirmed facts from uncertain estimates; update without discarding everything; keep it understandable and correctable, so users can inspect preferences, spot wrong assumptions and control use beyond the current session.

Preference drift is when a user's interests, requirements or priorities change over time, gradually or through sudden life events. Drift detection is essential for the reliability and safety of deployed AI. UX researchers should examine how responsive and appropriate model updates are.

Context-aware personalisation adjusts interfaces in real time to the situation, preferences and behaviour. Contextual signals should be limited to what is relevant to the task, adaptation should stay predictable, and the contribution of contextual information should be evaluated. Success factors: understand behaviour and put the user first; clear data-usage policies with privacy settings that are easy to understand and control; real-time interface updates.

Feedback loops, exposure bias, diversity and exploration

A personalisation feedback loop connects the system's recommendations with later user interactions. Feedback may be explicit or implicit and only matters if the system processes it appropriately. Unlike older static models on outdated data, loop-driven systems evolve with real-time interactions. Exposure bias: the recommender determines which items are seen, so clicks are conditional on exposure; with implicit feedback it influences users' choices and so its own future recommendations.

Diversity. The slide's diversity example compares two lists for the same goal. List A: low diversity, five business-analytics courses, Diversity@5 = 0.20 (1 category out of 5). List B: higher diversity, Diversity@5 = 0.60 (3 categories out of 5); its five courses are tagged business analytics, programming, data visualisation, career development and project/portfolio. Both lists stay relevant to Chelsea's goal (relevance 95% down to 90% in A, 95% down to 86% in B). Note that the slide's own picture shows five different tags in B while its label says 3 categories; quote the slide's 0.20 and 0.60. The metric is category coverage: D@K = distinct categories in the top K ÷ K. Benefit: users discover complementary skills and learning pathways and make more informed choices.

Exploration vs exploitation. The trade-off between discovering new strategies and using known successful ones. Exploitation recommends items currently estimated as highly relevant; exploration introduces options that teach the system about the user's interests. Too much exploitation reinforces narrow recommendations; too much exploration reduces immediate usefulness. The right strategy considers user context and the cost of exploration.

User control, feedback and boundaries

AI recommendations should support decisions, not unnecessarily replace them; choice needs access to relevant alternatives. A visible settings menu is not control: controls must meaningfully influence behaviour, let users revise preferences and correct assumptions, and show whether a change took effect. Override lets users reject AI suggestions or decisions. The right level of control depends on the task's consequences.

Feedback mechanisms. Different feedback actions mean different things; feedback should be specific, acknowledged without overstating its effect, not burdensome, and judged by whether it improves the next meaningful interaction.

Respecting boundaries. Personalisation works within user-defined and system-defined boundaries: separate hard constraints from ranking preferences, let users opt out of optional personalisation, collect data in proportion to the purpose, and explain boundaries in accessible language. Respecting consent and boundaries protects brand integrity and revenue, and can improve how users interpret AI behaviour.

A human-centred evaluation scorecard combines several dimensions: recommendation quality (suitable items retrieved and ranked well: Precision@K, NDCG, constraint compliance, diversity); task-level measures (do users achieve meaningful objectives); user understanding (are recommendations and adaptation comprehensible); control and safety (harmful or unacceptable behaviour). Why adaptive AI needs new UX research approaches

  • The mechanism matters. Traditional evaluation assumes similar actions give stable responses. With adaptive AI, evaluate the underlying mechanism as well as the visible interface; the number of possible experiences grows; product updates can change the experience without changing the layout; document both user conditions and system conditions. Researchers need technical fluency to separate algorithmic failures from interaction-design failures, without having to build the models.
  • Non-deterministic AI. A deterministic process gives the same result for the same relevant inputs and state; a non-deterministic one may differ under apparently identical conditions. Separate variation from plain incorrectness, decide what must stay stable, repeat trials, and document conditions: model version, prompt, available context, retrieval snapshot, tool responses, decoding configuration, evaluation rubric. Balance desirable flexibility against unacceptable instability.
  • Sessions, not single responses. A session has multiple related actions; errors arise at different stages; success must reflect the user's real objective; combine trajectory-level evidence with human outcomes. The slide's picture lists problems (irrelevance, low quality, high effort, cognitive load, many revisions) and solutions (flipped interaction, question refinement, collaborative task definition and assessment).
  • A/B testing (split testing) is a quantitative method: show different users two versions to see which performs better; A is the original, B the variation. Start from a testable hypothesis, assign randomly, define primary and secondary measures beforehand, interpret effect sizes with uncertainty. Adaptive systems add complications.
  • Causal inference. Does an intervention change an outcome? Observational comparisons may be confounded; before-and-after comparisons do not prove causation; recommenders add selection and exposure biases; randomised experiments are one important approach; observational corrections need explicit assumptions.
  • Longitudinal evaluation follows the same users or comparable cohorts over time to see whether the system stays useful as users gain experience and change goals. It can reveal preference drift, changing understanding of the personalisation, and delayed or cumulative effects; account for changes unrelated to the system; evaluate both stability and appropriate adaptation.
  • Behavioural telemetry is systematically recorded information about interactions. It shows patterns that retrospective interviews miss. Define events consistently, record enough context without unnecessary personal information, let privacy shape the architecture from the start, and link telemetry to meaningful UX questions.

Emerging trends and innovations in UX research

  • Human evaluation of generative AI. Assess how people experience and use AI outputs. Accuracy and helpfulness are different dimensions; check decision quality when outputs influence choices; rubrics need observable criteria; evaluators themselves can be biased and inconsistent; use realistic user requests and whole interactions. The slide's rubric rates 1, 3 and 5 on accuracy (major unsupported claims / mostly correct, minor issues / fully supported on checked facts), relevance (fails the task / partially relevant / directly addresses it), clarity (difficult / some ambiguity / clear and well structured) and actionability (no useful next step / limited guidance / supports an appropriate decision).
  • AI-assisted qualitative analysis. Generative AI can organise and give a first analysis of qualitative material, but themes must stay grounded in the evidence, humans review for accuracy and completeness, traceability links every finding to supporting data, an audit trail is kept, and the productivity gain must count verification effort. Industry evidence on the slide: a Nielsen Norman Group survey of 521 UX practitioners (published 23 October 2026) found 54% cited AI output quality as a barrier and 50% data privacy or security; it used a convenience sample, so the percentages are not population estimates.
  • Synthetic users. AI-generated stand-ins that simulate user responses. A synthetic response is not direct empirical evidence about human experience; synthetic data may mirror dominant patterns and underrepresent diversity, which harms subgroup analysis. Use them to complement valid human research, never to replace it.
  • Agentic AI. Systems that execute multi-step activities toward a user's goal (retrieve information, compare options, prepare documents, update records). Delegation becomes the central interaction: the user states an outcome and lets the AI choose some steps. It needs clear boundaries between assistance and authorisation, and visibility into ongoing actions.
  • Evaluating multi-step agents. Errors propagate (an outdated prerequisite corrupts later eligibility checks). Measure end-to-end task success separately from step success: nine correct steps followed by an unauthorised final submission is not success. Evaluate recovery (detect, locate, correct, avoid repeating consequential mistakes). Human supervision helps but excessive confirmations create effort and cancel the benefit of delegation. Consider accuracy, authorisation, recoverability and consequences, using task completion, tool-use correctness, intervention frequency, error severity and user understanding.
  • Generative user interfaces. AI creates or assembles interface elements for a request or context, extending adaptation from content selection to interface structure. The slide contrasts "today: the same interface for everyone" with "future: a personalised interface for each user". It brings reliability challenges, so evaluate generated interfaces as functioning interactions and test whether generation improves outcomes.

One project, carried through five tasks

HW 1 · UI and UX research for AI-powered solutions

  • Asked: pick a domain, find 3 existing apps with comparable functions (at least 1 with an AI feature), name the users and ≥2 key tasks. Build one evaluation checklist on one scale (e.g. 0 poor / 1 partial / 2 good) covering usability, UX, AI interaction and trustworthy AI. Evaluate all 3, then pick 2 significant UX problems, propose an AI fix for each with its risks, and draw 1 annotated wireframe.
  • Literature named: NN/g's 10 usability heuristics, Microsoft HAX guidelines for human–AI interaction, W3C WAI (accessibility), EU ALTAI (assessment list for trustworthy AI).
  • The conclusion must answer: which problems really need AI and which need plain UI fixes; who decides; what transparency and oversight users need; and give ≥2 measurable UX indicators.
Exam angle"Which of these problems needs AI?" A missing back button, a confusing form or inconsistent icons are UI fixes. AI earns its place only where the task needs prediction, recognition, recommendation or language, and plain UX cannot do it as well (the course's own principle).

HW 2 · User experience and needs identification

  • Asked: a real problem where AI could improve UX; ≥2 personas or roles (goals, responsibilities, motivations, expectations, challenges) built with ≥1 method: personas, empathy map, jobs-to-be-done (JTBD) or journey map. Then visualise user problems and solution options for 2 use cases.
  • Five questions about each problem: how widespread is it, who does it affect, how does it affect different people, how does it differ by context, does it change over time?
  • The methods are taught in lecture 04 (personas, empathy maps, journey maps) and reused in the Empathize and Define stages of lecture 05.

HW 3 · UI prototype and AI capability analysis

  • Automation vs augmentation brief: which activities AI should perform automatically, and which it should support rather than replace human decision-making. Lecture 07 defines augmentation: a machine "amplifies the user's own capabilities".
  • HITL vs HOTL general: in the loop = the human approves each consequential output; on the loop = the AI acts and the human supervises with the power to stop or override.
  • The prototype must show: a full main scenario, ≥1 alternative path, how AI output is presented, how users accept, reject, modify or ask for clarification, and handling of ≥1 AI error or uncertain output. Visual polish is secondary.
  • Reflection questions: how does the interaction improve the task; how are responsibilities split between AI and user; how does the UI stop decisions based on wrong or uncertain output; what lets users understand, control and correct the AI; which two measurable UX indicators; which ≥2 limitations?

HW 4 · Risk management of AI solution UIs

  • Asked: ≥2 AI functions (detection, prediction, recommendation, classification, decision support), their users and the decisions they influence; the value and risk of each; then an AI value matrix for one function: TP, TN, FP and FN as understandable real-world scenarios, the benefit or cost of each, a justified value or risk level per cell, whether FP or FN matters more and why, and how precision, recall or thresholds shift the trade-off.
  • UI matrix brief: for the same four outcomes, how the application should communicate each one to users, e.g. a confident alert for a likely TP, a softer "possible, please check" for uncertain cases, an easy "this was wrong" for FPs, and a visible fallback when the AI may have missed something.
  • Literature named: Shen et al. (2020) on confusion-matrix representations for non-experts; Google PAIR's What-If Tool tutorial on classifier performance; Nudelman (2025), UX for AI.

  • Submit: documentation (problem, users, why AI, AI functions, UI/UX decisions, human–AI interaction, user value matrix with metrics and thresholds), a working prototype URL, and references.

Pairs that get swapped

  • UX (the whole experience) vs UI (the interface you touch)
  • AI capability (what the model can do) vs AI usability (what users can do with it)
  • accuracy (the model) vs UX (whether users finish real tasks well)
  • persona (who) vs empathy map (says, thinks, does, feels) vs journey map (stages over time)
  • efficiency (objective effort) vs satisfaction (subjective feeling)
  • design thinking stages vs double-diamond phases ("Define" is in both)
  • POV statement (user + need + insight) vs HMW question (opens solutions)
  • early fusion (combine inputs) vs late fusion (combine recognisers' decisions)
  • AR (adds to the real world) vs VR (replaces it) vs MR (integrates both)
  • presence (the user's state) vs immersion (the technology's capability)
  • 3DoF (rotation) vs 6DoF (rotation + position)
  • augmentation (amplifies the human) vs automation (replaces the human)
  • personalisation (system-driven) vs customisation (user-driven)
  • explicit (declared) vs implicit (behavioural) preferences
  • hard constraints (filter first) vs soft preferences (rank after)

Answers the slides keep giving

  • "Does more X make it better?" No: more modalities, a newer framework, higher accuracy, a more impressive VR scene or one better metric do not by themselves improve the experience. The answer is always "for which user, task and context?".
  • "Which perspective can you ignore?" None (lecture 08, slide 53).
  • "What if the AI is wrong?" Show uncertainty, let the user verify, reject, correct or override, recover to a safe state, and keep consequential decisions with the human.
  • "Is it better?" Judge on several measures together (a scorecard), say which one got worse, and test the change with users or an experiment.

Three moves for a written question

  1. Name the user, task and context first. Every lecture's answer turns on them.
  2. Run the course's eight questions (Start tab): problem, why AI, could plain UX do it, what if wrong, how the user understands it, can they override, what stays human, how to measure.
  3. Close with a measurable indicator: task success, time, errors, satisfaction, trust or override rate, compared before and after.
